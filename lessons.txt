# Look Beyond the Surface

### How awards shape the future of hospitality — and how to use them to your advantage

A shift in guest behaviour rarely begins with a revolution. More often, it starts with small signals: where people choose to gather, what they are willing to wait for, what makes them return, and what they tell their friends afterwards.

Awards and rankings capture these signals in different ways. MICHELIN looks for culinary mastery and consistency. The World’s 50 Best reflects the experiences that resonate with a global community of industry professionals. GreatList evaluates a venue through expert audits that consider food, service and the customer journey, ergonomics, and atmosphere.

Understanding these differences matters. An award is never just a badge on a wall. It reflects a particular set of values, a particular evaluation method, and a particular idea of what excellence should look like.

Consider two very different expressions of excellence.

In Tokyo, Sukiyabashi Jiro became internationally recognised for its uncompromising approach to sushi. Its intimate counter, minimal setting, and intense focus on technique embody the Japanese concept of *shokunin*: a lifelong commitment to mastering one's craft. The experience is built around precision, discipline, and consistency rather than spectacle.

In St. Petersburg, El Copitas built its identity around something different: warmth, intimacy, and a shared sense of discovery. Founded by Igor Zernov and his partners, the bar transformed a hidden courtyard into a celebration of Mexican culture, with handmade copitas, carefully chosen objects, and a communal atmosphere. Its recognition on The World’s 50 Best Bars list demonstrated the international appeal of a concept rooted in personality and community.

One experience is defined by precision; the other, by connection. Both illustrate the same principle: **recognition follows a clear point of view, not the size of a venue or the scale of its budget.**

## 1. Learn to read the industry

* *

When I graduated, I knew I wanted to build my career in the bar industry. What I didn't understand yet was how much of that industry exists beyond the counter.

Technical skills, service, cost control, team management, ergonomics, and storytelling are essential. But they are only part of the picture. To understand where hospitality is heading, you also need to follow the ideas emerging at the intersection of design, agriculture, technology, sustainability, and culture.

Awards can be one of the fastest ways to develop this broader perspective.

Look beyond the winners. Study the shortlists, the judging criteria, the concepts being recognised, and the reasons behind their success. Together, they offer a snapshot of what the industry values — and what it may value next.

Local recognition can also become a stepping stone to international visibility. A national World Class competition can lead to a global final. A regional restaurant award can bring a venue to the attention of international media and industry professionals. A small design competition can introduce a space to a jury that would otherwise never encounter it.

But visibility rarely comes from waiting to be discovered.

If you believe your project deserves recognition, research the relevant awards. Read the entry requirements. Understand the criteria. Ask organisers for guidance, prepare your evidence, and apply when the opportunity is right.

**Treat awards not only as goals to pursue, but also as tools for learning, positioning, and professional growth.**

## 2. Not all awards play by the same rules

Before deciding which awards matter to your project, understand what each one actually measures.

The industry has several distinct recognition systems. Although their logos may appear side by side, their methods and purposes can be very different.

**Rankings and guides** build reputations over time by evaluating or voting on experiences. Examples include MICHELIN, The World’s 50 Best, GreatList, and The Pinnacle Guide.

**Jury-based awards** assess a defined body of work against specific criteria. These may include a venue, a menu, a design concept, a beverage programme, or a contribution to the industry. Examples include the Tales of the Cocktail Spirited Awards, the Restaurant & Bar Design Awards, Prix Versailles, and the James Beard Awards.

**Professional competitions** test individuals or teams against a particular brief, skill set, or competitive format. Diageo World Class and national bartender championships are examples.

The distinction is important because it determines how you can participate — and what you should do to prepare.

### The World’s 50 Best Bars

The main list is based on votes from an international academy of drinks experts. It is not an application-based competition: venues cannot simply submit themselves for consideration.

However, the wider awards programme includes categories such as Best Bar Design and Best Cocktail Menu, which have their own entry processes and assessment criteria.

The lesson? A venue cannot directly control every route to recognition, but it can identify opportunities within the wider ecosystem.

### Tales of the Cocktail Spirited Awards

The Spirited Awards recognise excellence across the global drinks industry through a nomination and judging process. Public nominations are accepted, self-nomination is encouraged, and the programme includes multiple professional and venue categories.

For eligible projects, this creates a more direct route to consideration: understand the categories, identify the strongest evidence of your work, and make a clear, well-supported nomination.

### The Pinnacle Guide

The Pinnacle Guide takes a more structured approach to evaluating bars. Its process includes a detailed application and anonymous assessments, with attention extending beyond drinks to areas such as team management, community engagement, and environmental practices.

This broader perspective reflects an important shift in hospitality: a great bar is not defined by its menu alone. The way it operates, supports its people, and relates to its surroundings is part of the experience.

### MICHELIN

MICHELIN recognition is based on anonymous inspections and the guide's established assessment process. Restaurants cannot apply to buy or secure a star through a submission.

For operators, the practical response is not to chase inspectors. It is to build a restaurant that consistently meets its own standards and delivers an exceptional experience.

### Start closer to home

National and regional awards can provide valuable first steps. Programmes such as the CLASS Bar Awards in the UK, the Scottish Bar & Pub Awards, and national World Class competitions can help emerging teams gain visibility, build professional relationships, and establish a track record.

The strategic question is not simply, "Which award is the most prestigious?"

It is: **Which award recognises the kind of excellence we are trying to build, and what is the most credible route to being considered?**

## 3. Objectivity is a method, not a logo

No award is entirely free from subjectivity. Different systems use different methods to define excellence, and each method has its strengths and limitations.

The useful question is not whether an award is perfectly objective. It is whether its process is transparent enough for you to understand what the result means.

When evaluating an award, ask five questions:

1. **Who evaluates the work?** Anonymous inspectors, industry peers, specialist judges, or a public vote?
2. **What is being measured?** Technical execution, consistency, originality, guest experience, design, or overall reputation?
3. **What evidence is considered?** Written submissions, documented practices, interviews, site visits, or professional experience?
4. **How is consistency assessed?** Through a single submission, repeated visits, or votes based on past experiences?
5. **How is the process funded?** Are there entry fees, commercial partnerships, sponsorships, or other financial relationships that should be understood?

These questions help you interpret results rather than simply accept a ranking at face value.

For example, MICHELIN's anonymous inspections focus on the quality and consistency of the dining experience within its established framework. The World’s 50 Best reflects the collective judgement of an international voting academy. The Pinnacle Guide uses a more structured assessment of a bar's overall operation. GreatList applies expert audits to elements including food, service, the customer journey, ergonomics, and atmosphere.

Each system provides a different lens.

A ranking can reveal what resonates with industry professionals. An inspection-based guide can signal sustained quality. An operational audit can highlight strengths and weaknesses that a popularity-based list may not capture.

**Learn what a recognition system measures before using its results to make decisions about your own project.**

## 4. What the latest recognition signals tell us

Awards become more valuable when you look for patterns across different categories rather than focusing on individual winners.

Several recent examples illustrate how excellence in hospitality is being expressed in increasingly diverse ways.

### Simplicity can be a competitive advantage

Hong Kong's Bar Leone, known for its neighbourhood-bar philosophy and its mortadella sandwich, retained the number-one position on The World’s 50 Best Bars list in 2025.

Its success challenges the assumption that premium recognition requires theatrical service, elaborate interiors, or complicated menus. A clear identity, strong execution, and genuine hospitality can be powerful differentiators.

### Hospitality is part of the product

Recognition for hospitality-focused concepts reinforces the idea that guests remember more than what they eat or drink. They remember how a place made them feel, how the team treated them, and whether the experience felt personal.

The implication for operators is straightforward: service should not be treated as an accessory to the product. It is part of the product.

### Sustainability is becoming a creative discipline

Athens-based Line has attracted international attention for its fermentation-led, low-waste approach. Its model shows how sustainability can influence flavour development, purchasing decisions, menu design, and the identity of a venue.

The opportunity is not to add a sustainability statement to an existing concept. It is to make resourcefulness and environmental responsibility part of how the concept works.

### Innovation must be meaningful

At the Spirited Awards, Singapore's FURA received recognition as World's Best Bar, bringing attention to a distinctive approach to ingredients and sustainability. Other recognised projects have explored alcohol-free beverage experiences, inventive cocktail menus, and spaces designed around music and listening.

These examples point to a broader principle: innovation matters most when it changes the guest experience or expresses a venue's identity more clearly.

A new ingredient, an unusual technique, or a striking interior is not automatically valuable. The real question is what it contributes to the experience — and whether it makes sense for that particular place.

### Design is more than decoration

A listening bar, a communal table, a carefully planned service station, and an accessible layout may look like different design challenges. In practice, they all influence how guests interact with a space and how efficiently the team can work.

Good design connects atmosphere with function. It supports the concept, guides movement, and makes the intended experience possible.

### The wider lesson

Taken together, these examples suggest that recognition increasingly extends beyond technical excellence alone. Identity, hospitality, sustainability, thoughtful design, and the coherence of the entire experience all contribute to how a project is perceived.

For the 2027 awards cycle, these are useful areas to watch — not guaranteed judging criteria, but credible directions for operators to investigate.

Look for demonstrable practice rather than impressive language: evidence of staff wellbeing, meaningful low- and no-alcohol options, responsible sourcing, accessible design, and a menu that communicates a distinctive point of view.

*Note: Award categories, winners, eligibility rules, and assessment methods change between editions. Always check the official programme before using a specific result or requirement in a submission.*

## 5. Build a project worth recognising

There is a difference between building a strong hospitality concept and building something that merely looks good in an award submission.

The first begins with a clear understanding of the guest, the team, the product, and the place. The second begins with a checklist of what the organisers might want to see.

The distinction matters.

A project built around a credible point of view can develop a consistent identity across its drinks, food, service, atmosphere, sourcing, and communication. A project built around an award may end up with disconnected features that look impressive individually but fail to create a coherent experience.

Instead of asking, "What do we need to add to win?", begin with better questions:

* **Identity:** What makes this place recognisable and difficult to replicate elsewhere?
* **Guest experience:** What should guests feel, remember, and tell others about?
* **Consistency:** Can the team deliver the intended experience on a quiet Tuesday as well as a busy Saturday?
* **People:** Does the operating model support the team that makes the experience possible?
* **Responsibility:** Are sustainability and community engagement reflected in actual decisions and practices?
* **Evidence:** Can we demonstrate our strengths through documented results, repeatable processes, and real examples?

Once you have clear answers, identify the awards that align with those strengths.

Then prepare deliberately. Read the criteria. Collect evidence. Document your concept. Explain why your decisions matter. Make the connection between what you believe, what you do, and what the guest experiences.

An award application should communicate the substance of your project, not disguise its weaknesses.

## Final thought

The most valuable thing about the awards circuit is not the list of names you learn or the badges you hope to collect. It is the ability to recognise what the industry is responding to — and to translate those insights into better decisions.

Study the winners, but also study the methods behind the recognition. Follow emerging categories. Question the assumptions behind rankings. Notice which ideas travel across markets and which remain rooted in local culture.

Then bring those observations back to your own venue, team, or career.

**Do not build a venue to fit an award. Build a venue with a clear purpose, a consistent experience, and a point of view that could only come from you.**

Recognition may follow. More importantly, you will have built something worth recognising.

# DESIGNING ATMOSPHERE & THE SWEET FAIRY TALE

*Atmosphere is not decoration. It is the cumulative effect of hundreds of deliberate choices — light, sound, materials, tableware, movement and service — working together to create a world the guest can feel.*

**LESSON 02 · 16 MIN**

EDITORIAL LESSON · CASE STUDIES · KEY IDEAS

## THE IDEA

### Look beyond the surface.

Imagine a bar or restaurant as a sweet fairy tale.

For two hours, the guest agrees to believe in a world you have created. The candlelight feels warm, the noise of the street fades away, the glass feels right in the hand, and every plate, fork and object on the table seems to belong to the same story.

The guest may never consciously analyse why the experience feels so good. They will not necessarily think about colour temperature, acoustic treatment or the texture of a table surface. But they will feel the result. They may stay longer, order another drink, return with friends or remember the experience long after leaving.

That is the art of designing atmosphere.

**You are not simply creating an interior. You are creating a state of mind the guest wants to inhabit.**

But every fairy tale is fragile.

A harsh overhead light, a cheap plastic tray, a dirty door handle, a phone ringing behind the bar or the constant noise of a receipt printer can instantly break the illusion. Suddenly, the guest is no longer immersed in your world. They are simply sitting in a room.

The problem is not always that a particular object looks unattractive. It is that the object disrupts the logic of the experience.

Atmosphere cannot be created through one impressive design decision. It emerges from hundreds of details working together.

Let's explore how leading hospitality professionals translate a point of view into a tangible guest experience.

## 01. WHEN SERVICE BECOMES ARCHITECTURE

### Simone Caporale · Sips, Barcelona

Sips, co-founded by Simone Caporale and his partners, has become a notable example of how a contemporary bar can rethink the relationship between guests, space and the craft of bartending.

Rather than relying entirely on the traditional counter as a barrier between the bartender and the guest, an open central workstation can create a more direct, visible interaction. Guests become observers of the craft, watching the preparation of drinks and the movements of the team.

Service becomes more than a sequence of actions. It becomes choreography.

The bartender's position, the trajectory of each movement, the timing of a presentation, eye contact and the shape of the vessel all contribute to the composition.

Glassware and custom-made vessels can become spatial accents in their own right. Unexpected forms turn the act of serving a drink into a memorable moment, adding character without necessarily relying on traditional symbols of luxury.

The objective is not to make every element expensive or unusual. It is to ensure that every element has a purpose.

**The key insight:** ergonomics and aesthetics are not opposites. When the working environment is designed well, the team can operate more effectively, and the guest gains a more engaging experience.

Consider the following questions when analysing a bar concept:

* What does the guest see in the first few seconds after taking a seat?
* Which actions of the team become part of the experience?
* What should be visible, and what should remain out of sight?
* Does the layout encourage interaction or create unnecessary barriers?

## 02. WHEN OBJECTS TELL A STORY

### Denis Bobkov · Black Swan & Bambule, Moscow

In Denis Bobkov's projects, atmosphere takes a different form. The space becomes a theatrical narrative.

Antique silverware, vintage porcelain, dripping wax candles, reclaimed doors and a sequence of hidden rooms can create the feeling of entering a world that existed long before the guest arrived.

The important element is not the individual object, but its relationship with everything around it.

An antique plate can look like random theatrical decoration. But when its shape, texture and character connect with the rest of the environment, it becomes part of the venue's visual language.

The same principle applies to lighting, furniture, textiles, music and even the way a server places a glass on the table.

When objects support a shared narrative, the guest does not need a verbal explanation of the concept. They begin to understand it through their senses.

This leads to an essential principle:

**Authenticity comes not from the quantity of vintage objects, but from how naturally they belong together.**

A room filled with antiques can still lack atmosphere. A space containing only a few carefully selected objects can feel far more convincing.

When developing a concept, consider:

* Which objects genuinely support the idea behind the venue?
* Do they share a coherent visual language?
* What details might guests notice and remember?
* Which elements feel accidental or inconsistent with the story?

## 03. FROM FIVE-STAR RITUAL TO CONTEMPORARY BAR CULTURE

### Alex Kratena · Artesian & Tayēr + Elementary

Alex Kratena's work demonstrates how differently an excellent hospitality experience can be expressed, depending on the concept.

Artesian is associated with a more expressive and refined approach to cocktail culture. Tayēr + Elementary offers a different interpretation of the contemporary bar, with its own identity, approach to interaction and format of service.

The lesson is not that one style is better than another. It is that atmosphere must reflect the promise a venue makes to its guests.

If the concept calls for ceremony, the team's movements, the pace of service, the glassware and the lighting can all contribute to a sense of occasion.

If the concept is built around informality, overly elaborate rituals may create unnecessary distance.

There is no universal formula for the perfect atmosphere.

There is only the relationship between the idea, the space, the service and the expectations of the guest.

**Good design does not force a venue to appear different. It helps the venue express its own character more clearly.**

## 04. FIVE SENSES. ONE STORY.

Designing atmosphere requires looking beyond visual aesthetics.

Guests experience a space through sight, sound, smell, touch and taste. These sensory inputs interact with one another, shaping the overall impression of a place.

### Light

Light influences where guests look, what they notice and how they perceive a space.

Warm, localised lighting can create intimacy. Directional light can highlight a drink or a working area. Harsh overhead lighting, by contrast, may undermine the feeling of privacy and comfort.

There is no single correct colour temperature or brightness level for every venue. The right solution depends on the concept, the tasks performed by the team, safety requirements and guest comfort.

### Sound

Music establishes the emotional tone of a space, but acoustics are equally important.

Even a carefully selected playlist cannot compensate for a room where guests have to shout to hear one another, or where kitchen noise and operational sounds constantly interrupt the experience.

Consider not only the genre of music, but also volume, tempo, room acoustics and how the soundscape changes throughout the evening.

### Scent

Scent can trigger associations and influence how a space is perceived.

However, intentionally introduced fragrances must be subtle and appropriate. In venues where the aroma of food and drinks is central to the experience, additional scents can interfere with the guest's ability to appreciate the product.

Sometimes the best solution is not to add a fragrance, but to eliminate unwanted smells.

### Touch

Material textures often go unnoticed consciously, yet they influence our perception of quality.

The weight of a glass, the smoothness of ceramic, the surface of a table, the feel of a napkin and the temperature of metal all contribute to the venue's tactile language.

When selecting objects and materials, consider not only how they look, but also how they feel in the hand and perform in everyday use.

### Taste and presentation

Taste remains central to a food and beverage experience, but context influences how it is perceived.

The shape of a glass, the temperature of a drink, its position on the table and the timing of its presentation can enhance the experience or distract from it.

The goal is balance: tableware and service rituals should support the product rather than compete with it for attention.

**The guiding principle:** the five senses should not compete with one another. They should reinforce the same concept.

## 05. THE BACK OF HOUSE MUST NOT BREAK THE SPELL

Atmosphere is shaped not only by what guests see, but also by what they never notice.

Imagine a beautiful bar with soft lighting, thoughtful tableware and carefully selected music. Suddenly, an irritated conversation comes from behind the counter, a box of supplies appears in view, and a receipt printer begins producing a constant stream of noise.

The physical space has not changed. The experience has.

The guest is now confronted with operational details that do not fit the world the venue has created.

This does not mean the work of the team should always be hidden. In some concepts, an open kitchen or visible cocktail preparation is central to the experience.

The distinction lies in what is revealed and how it is presented.

If bartending is meant to be visible, make the process engaging and the workstation functional. If stock storage is not part of the concept, organise it so that it does not intrude into the guest's field of vision.

Look at the space from the perspective of someone visiting for the first time.

* Are cables, boxes, waste or unrelated objects visible?
* Can unnecessary technical noise be eliminated?
* Is there clutter on working surfaces?
* Do staff movements create avoidable interruptions?
* Does the team's appearance support the atmosphere?

The back of house does not necessarily need to be invisible. It needs to be organised in a way that supports the overall experience.

## 06. ATMOSPHERE UNFOLDS OVER TIME

One of the most common mistakes in hospitality design is treating atmosphere as a static image.

But guests do not experience a venue as a photograph. They experience it as a sequence of moments.

They approach the entrance, open the door, look around, meet a member of the team, take a seat, receive their first drink, spend time in the space and eventually leave.

Each stage shapes their expectations of what comes next.

For this reason, atmosphere should be designed as a sequence of scenes.

### Arrival

The first few seconds introduce the character of the venue.

What does the guest see, hear and feel? How are they welcomed? Does the experience make them want to stay?

### The defining moment

This is where the concept reveals itself most clearly.

It could be a cocktail prepared in front of the guest, an unexpected presentation, a musical moment or the feeling of intimacy around a shared table.

### Farewell

The final moments are also part of the experience.

How does the interaction end? Does the guest feel that they have been looked after? Is there a detail they will remember or want to share with someone else?

These scenes do not need to be theatrical. Sometimes the most memorable moment is a quiet, thoughtful gesture from a team member who understands exactly what the guest needs.

What matters is that every stage expresses the same underlying idea.

## CONCLUSION

Atmosphere is the result of a coordinated relationship between space, objects, sound, light and people.

It cannot be created through an expensive renovation, striking glassware or a collection of vintage objects alone. Even the most beautiful details lose their impact when they fail to support a coherent concept.

Study the work of Simone Caporale, Denis Bobkov and Alex Kratena not to copy their decisions, but to understand how different philosophies can be translated into spatial design and service.

The challenge for any designer, owner or manager is to define the experience their venue should create and then express that idea consistently through everything the guest encounters.

Do not ask only whether a space looks beautiful.

Ask what a person feels inside it, why they feel that way, and which details help sustain that feeling throughout the evening.

Ultimately, a guest may forget the colour of the walls or the shape of a light fixture.

But they will remember **how they felt in your space.**

That is the true power of atmosphere.


# THE SENSES AT THE TABLE, VESSEL WEIGHT & FLAVOUR ARCHITECTURE

**LESSON 03 · 16 MIN**

*Flavour is more than taste. The weight of antique silver, the clarity of crystal, candlelit shadows, aromatic herbs, texture and sound all influence how we experience food and drink.*

## THE IDEA

### Look beyond the surface.

A guest begins experiencing a dish or drink long before the first sip or bite. The weight of a spoon, the temperature of a glass, the sound of ice, the aroma of freshly crushed herbs and the colour of a drink all shape the brain’s perception of flavour.

This is the principle explored by **gastrophysics**, a field associated with Charles Spence that examines how our senses interact with food and drink. Taste is never an isolated experience. It is constructed through the combined influence of sight, smell, sound, touch, memory and expectation.

For hospitality professionals, this opens up a powerful creative opportunity: the ability to design not only what guests consume, but how they perceive, anticipate and remember it.

A signature serve is not simply a recipe. It is a coordinated sensory experience.

## 01 — FLAVOUR AS A MULTISENSORY EXPERIENCE

### The four coordinates of a signature serve

Bartender and mixologist Artem Talalay, a Diageo Reserve World Class Russia winner and Palm Branch Hall of Fame bartender, approaches contemporary mixology through four interconnected coordinates:

**Taste. Aroma. Texture. Colour.**

Together, these elements create the identity of a drink.

**Taste** establishes the balance between sweetness, acidity, bitterness, salinity and other flavour dimensions. Every component should contribute to a clear overall direction.

**Aroma** creates anticipation and adds complexity. Fresh botanicals, citrus oils, spices and aromatic vapours can shape the experience before the drink reaches the palate.

**Texture** determines how a drink feels in the mouth. Carbonation, viscosity, temperature, dilution and the presence of ice all influence its character.

**Colour** establishes the first visual impression. A drink’s colour should support its identity, the ingredients it suggests and the wider concept of the venue.

These four coordinates are most effective when they work together. A drink may taste excellent, but if its aroma, texture, presentation or colour contradicts its concept, the overall experience can feel incomplete.

The same principle applies to food. A dish is not only a combination of ingredients; it is also a visual composition, an aromatic experience and a physical interaction with the guest.

## 02 — THE VESSEL IS PART OF THE FLAVOUR

### Weight, material and perceived value

A glass or plate is never entirely neutral. The object through which a guest experiences a product can influence expectations, perceived quality and the emotional meaning of the moment.

A heavy antique silver spoon may suggest tradition, craftsmanship and richness. Thin crystal can communicate delicacy and precision. Handmade ceramics introduce irregularity, tactility and a sense of individuality. Vintage cut glass can evoke another era, making even a simple drink feel connected to a larger story.

This is where tableware becomes part of flavour architecture.

The vessel influences several aspects of the experience:

* **Weight:** changes the perceived solidity, quality and importance of an object.
* **Material:** communicates temperature, craftsmanship, tradition or modernity.
* **Shape:** influences how a product is held, approached and presented.
* **Transparency:** reveals colour, clarity, bubbles and layers.
* **Texture:** creates a tactile impression before the guest tastes anything.
* **Sound:** introduces another sensory dimension through the placement of cutlery, the contact of glass and the movement of ice.

The objective is not to use expensive or antique objects simply because they look impressive. Every object should have a reason to exist within the concept.

A rustic ceramic cup may be more appropriate for a botanical cocktail bar than an elaborate crystal glass. A polished silver spoon may reinforce the identity of a formal dining room, while a deliberately unconventional vessel may communicate experimentation.

**The right vessel does not merely contain the product. It helps explain what the product is meant to be.**

## 03 — PRECISION BEFORE PERFECTION

### Dave Arnold and the science of drink development

In *Liquid Intelligence*, Dave Arnold approaches cocktail development through measurement, experimentation and an understanding of the physical processes behind a drink.

Temperature, dilution, carbonation and the balance between acidity and sweetness should not be treated as variables to adjust randomly. They can be measured, tested and refined.

This changes the way a team develops a menu.

Instead of relying exclusively on intuition or trying to recreate a successful drink from memory, the team can establish a repeatable method:

* Control the temperature of the ingredients and the finished drink.
* Measure dilution and understand how melting ice changes the balance.
* Test carbonation and observe how it affects aroma, texture and freshness.
* Adjust acidity and sweetness systematically.
* Change one variable at a time to understand its actual effect.
* Document the final specification so the drink can be reproduced consistently.

Precision does not eliminate creativity. It gives creativity a reliable foundation.

A drink that tastes exceptional once but changes significantly between bartenders is not yet a fully developed signature serve. The goal is to combine sensory ambition with operational consistency.

This principle extends beyond cocktails to coffee, tea, fermentation, pastry, sauces and almost every area of food and beverage development.

## 04 — WHEN AROMA BECOMES A PERFORMANCE

### Hiroyasu Kayama and Bar Benfiddich, Tokyo

At Bar Benfiddich in Tokyo, Hiroyasu Kayama is known for a deeply hands-on approach to botanical mixology. Fresh herbs and other ingredients are worked with traditional tools, including a mortar and pestle, while drinks may be served in antique apothecary-style glassware.

The preparation itself becomes part of the experience.

The sound of herbs being crushed, their aroma released into the air and the sight of the bartender working directly in front of the guest all create anticipation. The guest begins forming an impression of the drink before tasting it.

This is an important distinction: the preparation is not simply something that happens before the product is delivered. It can be an intentional part of the product’s identity.

Aromatic rituals can serve several purposes:

* They introduce the ingredients before the first sip.
* They create a sense of freshness and immediacy.
* They give the guest something to observe and remember.
* They make craftsmanship visible.
* They connect the drink to the philosophy of the venue.

However, a ritual should never become performance without purpose. Every movement should support the flavour, the story or the emotional tone of the experience.

The most compelling service rituals feel natural to the concept. They do not look like entertainment added after the fact.

## 05 — TEMPERATURE, TIMING AND THE ART OF SEQUENCING

### Jiro Ono and Sukiyabashi Jiro, Tokyo

The work of sushi master Jiro Ono illustrates how precision extends beyond ingredients and presentation into temperature, timing and the sequence of service.

At Sukiyabashi Jiro, the relationship between the sushi rice, the fish, the seasoning and the moment of serving is carefully controlled. The rice is traditionally served close to body temperature, although the exact temperature varies with the preparation and conditions.

The experience also depends on timing. Each piece arrives as part of a carefully paced sequence, with the chef’s finishing gestures and attention to the individual guest contributing to the overall impression.

This connects to the **peak–end rule**: people often remember an experience disproportionately through its most emotionally significant moment and the way it ends.

For a hospitality venue, this has practical implications.

A tasting menu, cocktail flight or multi-course dinner should not be considered merely a list of products. It is a progression of experiences. Each moment can prepare the guest for the next, while the final interaction can shape the memory of the entire visit.

A strong sequence considers:

* The rhythm between courses or drinks.
* Changes in intensity, temperature and texture.
* Moments of surprise or contrast.
* The amount of time guests need to appreciate each stage.
* The emotional quality of the final impression.

The objective is not to make every moment equally dramatic. It is to create a rhythm in which each element has a purpose.

## 06 — THE FIVE SENSES, ONE COHERENT STORY

A signature dish or drink exists within a larger sensory environment. Its effect is influenced by the lighting, music, tableware, materials, service style and surrounding atmosphere.

**Sight** establishes the first impression through colour, shape, composition and lighting.

**Smell** creates anticipation and connects the guest to ingredients, memories and associations.

**Sound** contributes to atmosphere through music, conversation, the movement of ice, the preparation of food and the rhythm of service.

**Touch** communicates through vessel weight, surface texture, temperature, cutlery and the physical qualities of the food or drink.

**Taste** brings the experience together through flavour, balance, texture and aftertaste, while being influenced by the other senses.

The most effective hospitality concepts do not design these senses independently. They coordinate them.

Imagine a botanical cocktail bar built around the idea of an apothecary. The guest encounters warm, low lighting, dark timber, tactile glassware and the scent of freshly prepared herbs. The bartender crushes botanicals at the counter, creating an audible moment of preparation. The drink arrives in a vessel that feels connected to the room, with a flavour profile that reflects the ingredients just introduced.

Each detail reinforces the others. Nothing needs to explain the concept explicitly because the guest experiences it through a series of consistent sensory cues.

This is flavour architecture: the deliberate coordination of a product, its presentation and the environment in which it is experienced.

## 07 — FROM A SINGLE SERVE TO THE IDENTITY OF A VENUE

The same principles can guide the development of an entire hospitality concept.

A restaurant might express its identity through handmade plates, restrained lighting, the sound of open-fire cooking and the aroma of bread emerging from the kitchen.

A contemporary cocktail bar might use sculptural glassware, precise temperature control, aromatic preparation and a carefully choreographed service sequence.

A tea room might build its atmosphere around the warmth of ceramic cups, the sound of pouring water, delicate aromas and a slower rhythm of interaction.

In each case, the sensory experience should grow from the central idea of the venue.

The menu, tableware, interior, music and service rituals should feel as though they belong to the same world. When these elements contradict one another, the concept becomes less coherent. When they reinforce one another, even small details can become memorable.

The goal is not sensory excess. It is **sensory intention**.

A single carefully chosen vessel, a distinctive aroma or a perfectly timed gesture may communicate more than a room filled with decorative details.

## THE TAKEAWAY

Flavour is not created in the kitchen or behind the bar alone. It emerges through the relationship between the product, the senses, the objects used to serve it and the context in which it is experienced.

Gastrophysics helps us understand how perception works. Dave Arnold demonstrates the value of systematic experimentation. Artem Talalay’s four coordinates offer a framework for developing a signature serve. Hiroyasu Kayama shows how aroma and preparation can become part of the performance. Jiro Ono demonstrates the importance of precision, timing and the final impression.

Together, these ideas point towards a broader principle of hospitality design:

**Every sensory detail is an opportunity to reinforce the identity of the experience.**

The weight of the glass, the temperature of the drink, the sound of preparation, the aroma released at the counter and the rhythm of service are not isolated choices. They are components of one carefully designed story.

A successful venue does not simply serve food and drink. It creates an experience that guests can recognise, feel and remember.

# CONCEPT MANIFESTOS & DECONSTRUCTING THE WORLD’S 50 BEST MENUS

**MODULE 04 · RESTAURANT & BAR CONCEPTS**

**LESSON 04 · 18 MIN**

*In the world’s leading bars and restaurants, a menu is never just a list of products and prices. It is the concept made tangible — a physical object that communicates the identity of a venue, sets expectations and shapes the guest experience.*

## THE IDEA

### Look beyond the surface.

A compelling hospitality concept brings together its audience, occasion, offer, space and business model under one unmistakable idea. That idea should be recognisable not only in the interior or the service style, but also in the moment a guest opens the menu.

A menu is one of the first physical interactions between a guest and a venue. Before ordering anything, the guest begins to understand the establishment’s personality through its language, layout, materials, visual references and organisation of choice.

Is the venue playful or serious? Contemporary or nostalgic? Experimental or familiar? Is it designed for a quick aperitivo, a long tasting experience or an evening of discovery?

The menu can answer these questions without explaining the concept directly.

Looking at the bars and restaurants associated with *The World’s 50 Best*, we can identify several distinct approaches to menu design. Some transform the menu into an art object. Others use it as a living archive, a research notebook or a declaration of neighbourhood identity.

The common principle is that **the menu should not merely describe the concept. It should embody it.**

## 01 — THE MENU AS AN ART MANIFESTO

### Rémy Savage: Little Red Door, A Bar with Shapes for a Name & Bar Nouveau

Rémy Savage’s work demonstrates how a single conceptual idea can influence the entire guest experience, from the way a menu is read to the way a drink is presented.

At Little Red Door in Paris, the menu has explored visual storytelling through formats such as illustrated narratives and architectural drawings. Instead of relying exclusively on conventional cocktail descriptions, this approach invites guests to interpret images, associations and emotions.

At A Bar with Shapes for a Name in London, geometric forms and Bauhaus-inspired design establish a recognisable visual language. Shapes such as circles, triangles and squares become more than decoration: they provide a framework for the identity of the menu and the wider environment.

At Bar Nouveau, references to Art Nouveau connect the experience to a different design tradition, drawing on the expressive lines and craftsmanship associated with the movement.

These examples illustrate an important principle: a menu becomes powerful when its design follows a clear conceptual rule.

That rule can influence:

* The graphic language and typography.
* The shape, format and physical construction of the menu.
* The naming and presentation of drinks.
* The choice of glassware and tableware.
* The materials and furniture used in the space.
* The way staff introduce the concept to guests.

The objective is not simply to create an attractive menu. It is to build a coherent world in which every element appears to belong.

**A strong concept turns design decisions into a system rather than a collection of unrelated aesthetic choices.**

## 02 — THE MENU AS A NEIGHBOURHOOD ARCHIVE

### Bar Leone, Hong Kong

Bar Leone has become known for its interpretation of Italian neighbourhood bar culture, expressed through the idea of *Cocktail Popolari* and an emphasis on accessible, familiar drinks.

The neighbourhood-bar approach is built around recognisability. It evokes the places where people return regularly, where the offer feels understandable and where hospitality is rooted in everyday rituals rather than exclusivity.

A menu built around this philosophy does not need to appear complicated to communicate sophistication. Its strength comes from knowing exactly what it wants to be.

The language should be direct. The offer should be easy to navigate. The drinks should feel connected to a clear cultural reference, while the execution can still demonstrate technical skill and attention to detail.

This is where simplicity becomes a deliberate design decision.

A familiar drink can become distinctive through ingredient quality, precise preparation, thoughtful presentation and a strong point of view. The menu does not have to make the product appear more complicated than it is.

For a venue developing its own identity, this raises an important question: what should the guest recognise immediately, and what should they discover gradually?

The strongest concepts often balance both. They offer an accessible point of entry while leaving room for depth, personality and repeat visits.

## 03 — THE MENU AS A LIVING DOCUMENT

### Igor Zernov: El Copitas and Tagliatella Caffe

A printed menu communicates stability. A changing menu communicates movement.

At El Copitas in St Petersburg, a venue associated with Mexican-inspired cocktail culture, the use of a frequently updated chalkboard menu has reflected an approach in which the offer can respond to ingredients, batches and the evolving work of the team.

A living menu makes change visible. It can create a sense of immediacy and encourage guests to return because the offer is not entirely fixed.

However, this format requires operational discipline. A menu that changes frequently must still be legible, coherent and easy for staff to explain. Its flexibility should feel intentional rather than improvised.

A different occasion calls for a different format. At a café or aperitivo-oriented venue such as Tagliatella Caffe, a menu can work more like a postcard: a compact expression of place, mood and a particular way of spending time.

The format should reflect how the venue is used.

A chalkboard may suit an evolving, hands-on bar. A small printed card may communicate a relaxed everyday ritual. A carefully bound booklet may support a longer, more considered dining experience.

Before choosing the visual format, consider the operational reality behind it:

* How often will the offer change?
* Who is responsible for updating the menu?
* How quickly must guests understand the options?
* Does the format work under the venue’s lighting conditions?
* Can staff explain changes consistently?
* Does the physical object suit the pace and occasion of service?

The most successful menu format is not necessarily the most elaborate. It is the one that supports the concept while working reliably in practice.

## 04 — THE MENU AS A RESEARCH NOTEBOOK

### Tuju, São Paulo

Tuju, the São Paulo restaurant recognised for its nature-led and research-oriented approach, provides an example of how a tasting menu can communicate a way of thinking rather than simply list courses.

Environmental conditions, seasonality and the behaviour of natural systems can become part of the narrative of a meal. Themes such as rain, wind and drought offer a framework through which ingredients, techniques and courses can be understood.

This approach turns the menu into a record of investigation.

Instead of presenting a sequence of unrelated dishes, the restaurant can guide guests through a central question or idea. Each course becomes part of a larger argument, with the ingredients and techniques contributing to the story.

The same principle can be applied beyond fine dining.

A cocktail menu might explore the relationship between fermentation and preservation. A wine bar could organise its selection around landscapes and growing conditions. A café might tell the story of a particular region through its producers, ingredients and preparation methods.

The key is that the narrative must be supported by the actual offer.

A theme cannot compensate for an incoherent menu. If the concept promises an exploration of climate, place or seasonality, the products, descriptions and sequence should make that exploration tangible.

**The story is strongest when the menu gives guests a framework for understanding what they are experiencing.**

## 05 — TWO FORMATS, TWO OCCASIONS

### Alex Kratena & Monica Berg: Tayēr + Elementary

Tayēr + Elementary in London illustrates how a hospitality business can bring different experiences together under one broader philosophy.

The venue has been associated with a dual-format approach: Elementary offers a more accessible, everyday bar experience, while Tayēr places greater emphasis on experimentation, ingredients and cocktail development.

The distinction is not simply about having two menus. It is about recognising that different occasions call for different kinds of hospitality.

A guest dropping in for a quick drink has different expectations from someone seeking a more exploratory cocktail experience. The level of explanation, the pace of service, the complexity of the offer and the amount of attention required may all differ.

A well-designed concept can accommodate these differences without losing its identity.

This requires clarity around several questions:

* What is the primary purpose of each format?
* Who is each experience designed for?
* How much time is the guest expected to spend?
* What level of exploration does the menu invite?
* How do the two formats relate without becoming interchangeable?

This principle is useful when developing a venue with several service modes, such as a restaurant with a bar, a café that becomes a wine bar in the evening, or a food-led venue with both a casual and a tasting-menu offer.

The formats should complement one another, with each having a clear role.

## 06 — FROM A SINGLE VENUE TO A PORTFOLIO OF CONCEPTS

### Boris Zarkov: White Rabbit Family and IKRA

At the scale of a hospitality group, concept development becomes a different challenge.

A group may operate several restaurants with different audiences, cuisines, price points and design languages. The challenge is to build a portfolio that can grow without making every venue feel like a variation of the same idea.

White Rabbit Family and its wider work, including the IKRA gastronomic platform, provide a useful starting point for considering how entrepreneurial vision can extend across multiple hospitality initiatives.

The key distinction is between a shared philosophy and a repeated identity.

A portfolio may share standards, expertise, ambition or a particular approach to hospitality. Individual venues, however, still need their own reasons to exist.

Each concept should be able to answer:

* Who is it for?
* What occasion does it serve?
* What makes its offer distinctive?
* What is its defining ritual or experience?
* Why would a guest choose it instead of another venue in the same group?

This applies even to a small independent operator planning a first venue and a future second location.

A concept should be specific enough to be recognisable, but structured enough to guide decisions as the business develops.

## 07 — THE ZERO-MENU APPROACH

### Hiroyasu Kayama: Bar Benfiddich

Not every hospitality experience needs to begin with a conventional printed menu.

At Bar Benfiddich in Tokyo, the bartender’s ingredient knowledge, hands-on preparation and interaction with the guest can become central to discovering the offer. The experience is shaped through conversation, visible craft and the sensory presence of the ingredients.

This suggests an alternative model: instead of presenting every choice in advance, the venue allows the guest to discover the offer through interaction.

But a minimal or absent menu does not mean an absence of structure.

The team must still be able to explain the available options, understand guest preferences, communicate prices and accommodate relevant dietary requirements. The experience should feel personal without becoming confusing or opaque.

A zero-menu or verbally guided approach is best suited to a concept where direct interaction is part of the promise.

For other venues, a hybrid format may be more effective: a concise printed menu supported by recommendations, seasonal specials or a guided conversation.

The choice should be determined by the guest experience, not by the desire to appear unconventional.

## 08 — MENU PSYCHOLOGY & ARCHITECTURE

### Designing choice without overwhelming the guest

A menu is also a decision-making tool.

Its structure influences how guests scan the offer, compare options and decide what to order. Too many choices can make the decision feel difficult; too few may limit discovery or fail to communicate the breadth of the concept.

There is no universal ideal number of items. A compact cocktail menu might work well with a focused selection, while a large restaurant requires a structure suited to its cuisine, service model and audience. As a starting point, a selection of around 8–14 core items can be useful for a focused bar menu, but the right number depends on the concept.

The more important question is whether every item has a clear role.

A menu can be organised around several different principles:

**By product type:** cocktails, wines, snacks, mains or desserts.

**By flavour:** bright and fresh, bitter and herbal, rich and savoury.

**By mood or occasion:** aperitivo, celebratory, slow evening or after-dinner.

**By origin:** regions, producers, ingredients or cultural traditions.

**By narrative:** chapters, seasons, landscapes or stages of a larger story.

Each structure encourages a different way of choosing. The organisation should help guests navigate the offer while reinforcing the venue’s identity.

### Naming and language

Names establish expectations before guests know exactly what a product contains.

A descriptive name communicates information directly. A poetic name creates curiosity but may require an explanation. A cultural reference can add depth when it is relevant and handled with care.

The most effective menus balance personality with clarity.

If every item has an abstract name, the guest may struggle to understand the offer. If every description is purely technical, the menu may feel generic. A clear naming system allows the venue to express character without sacrificing usability.

### Pricing presentation

The way prices appear also contributes to the experience.

Consistent alignment, clear typography and a readable hierarchy make a menu easier to use. Currency symbols, decimal conventions and the placement of prices should follow a deliberate system appropriate to the market and venue.

Some restaurants avoid prominent currency symbols or separate price columns to reduce visual emphasis on cost. This is a design choice, not a universal rule. Transparency remains essential: guests should be able to understand what they are ordering and what it costs before committing.

The objective is to create a menu that feels considered without making important information difficult to find.

### Material and tactility

The menu is an object guests hold, open, turn and place on the table.

Paper weight, surface texture, binding, edge treatment and durability all contribute to the impression it creates. A menu printed on textured stock may suggest craft and intimacy. A simple uncoated card can feel contemporary and direct. A durable, easy-to-clean format may be more appropriate for a busy neighbourhood bar.

Material choices should also reflect the practical conditions of service.

A beautiful menu that stains immediately, becomes difficult to read in low light or is expensive to replace may not be the right solution.

The physical object must balance concept, usability and operational reality.

## 09 — ONE CONCEPTUAL RULE, MANY DESIGN DECISIONS

The most coherent menus are built from a clear central principle.

If the concept is inspired by botanical research, the menu might resemble a field notebook, with concise ingredient information and a restrained visual language. The same idea could inform the glassware, the way herbs are prepared, the materials used in the interior and the language of service.

If the venue is built around Italian neighbourhood culture, the menu might be direct, compact and familiar, with an emphasis on classic drinks, accessible food and an atmosphere that encourages regular visits.

If the concept explores geometry and modernist design, the menu’s composition, naming system, furniture and glassware could all follow a consistent geometric vocabulary.

The goal is not to repeat the same motif everywhere. It is to ensure that each design decision supports the same underlying idea.

This creates coherence without requiring every element to look identical.

A strong menu concept should be recognisable even when reduced to its simplest form. Its logic should be understandable to the guest and useful to the team responsible for delivering the experience.

## THE TAKEAWAY

The menu is one of the most important objects in a hospitality venue because it connects concept, product, design and decision-making.

The work of Rémy Savage demonstrates how a conceptual rule can shape the visual identity of a menu. Bar Leone shows the strength of a clear neighbourhood-bar philosophy. El Copitas illustrates how a menu can remain responsive to the work happening behind the bar. Tuju demonstrates the potential of a narrative framework. Tayēr + Elementary highlights the importance of designing for different occasions, while the broader work of hospitality groups raises the question of how to preserve distinct identities as a business grows.

These examples do not offer a single formula to copy. They offer different ways to think about the relationship between a concept and its physical expression.

When developing a menu, begin with the central idea. Decide who the venue serves, what occasion it creates and what makes its offer distinctive. Then allow those decisions to guide the menu’s structure, language, materials, pricing presentation and relationship to the wider environment.

**A menu should feel as though it could belong only to that venue.**

It should make the concept tangible, help guests navigate the offer and give the team a practical tool for delivering the experience consistently.

# CONCEPT OBJECTS: THE MENU, THE MERCHANDISE & THE FURNITURE

**MODULE 04 · RESTAURANT & BAR CONCEPTS**

**LESSON 05 · 17 MIN**

*A concept becomes real when it leaves the wall and enters the guest’s hands — as a menu, a coaster, a cup, a stool or a bar station designed specifically for the way the venue works.*

## THE IDEA

### Look beyond the surface.

A hospitality concept does not exist only in a moodboard, a presentation or an interior design. It becomes tangible through the objects guests touch, use, see and sometimes take home.

A menu communicates the offer. A cup carries the identity of the brand beyond the venue. A coaster can become a small piece of storytelling. A chair influences how a guest occupies the space. A bar station determines how the team moves, prepares drinks and interacts with guests.

These objects may differ in scale, cost and purpose, but each contributes to the same question:

**How does the concept become a physical experience?**

Designing concept objects requires more than choosing attractive materials or adding a logo. Every object should connect three dimensions: the identity of the venue, the experience of the guest and the practical realities of operating a hospitality business.

The strongest objects feel distinctive because they belong to a particular concept. They also work because someone has considered how they will be made, used, maintained, replaced and paid for.

## 01 — THE MENU AS A KEEPSAKE

### CooCoo Coffee: Coffee · Croffles · Cookies

A menu can do more than explain what is available. It can become a recognisable expression of the brand.

CooCoo Coffee illustrates how a compact offer built around three memorable categories — coffee, croffles and cookies — can create a clear identity. The simplicity of the product language can extend across cups, window graphics, packaging and printed menus.

The principle is straightforward: when the core idea is clear, its physical expressions become easier to coordinate.

A cup does not need to carry the entire brand story. A menu does not need to explain every aspect of the business. Instead, each object reinforces the same recognisable language through its words, colours, typography, materials and proportions.

This consistency helps the brand travel beyond the venue. A guest carrying a cup or taking home a package continues to interact with the concept after leaving the space.

### Designing an object people want to keep

A takeaway object becomes memorable when it offers more than branding.

It might have an unusual shape, a satisfying texture, a useful format, a distinctive illustration or a message that captures the spirit of the venue. Its value can be emotional as much as functional.

Possible concept objects include:

* A beautifully designed menu card that guests keep as a souvenir.
* A cup or reusable tumbler with a recognisable visual identity.
* A coaster featuring a graphic, phrase or reference unique to the venue.
* A box or bag that turns takeaway packaging into part of the experience.
* A postcard, matchbook-style keepsake or small printed object that tells a story.

The objective is not to produce merchandise for its own sake. It is to identify an object that naturally belongs to the guest experience.

A coffee shop may express its identity through its cup and takeaway packaging. A cocktail bar might create a distinctive coaster or small printed drinks card. A destination restaurant could offer a beautifully produced menu or illustrated card connected to its culinary philosophy.

The right object depends on what the venue wants guests to remember.

## 02 — THE BAR STATION AS A DESIGNED OBJECT

### Pacific: Bar stations, mobile consoles and technical drawings

The bar is one of the most demanding working environments in hospitality. It must support speed, accuracy, cleanliness, storage and movement, often within a very limited footprint.

Yet the bar station is frequently treated as a decorative element first and a working tool second.

A more considered approach begins with the workflow.

Where does the bartender reach for ice? Where are the bottles positioned? How are glasses stored? Where does the waste go? How much movement is required to prepare a drink? Can the station support several people working at once without creating unnecessary collisions?

Specialist bar solutions, such as those associated with Pacific, demonstrate how these questions can be translated into purpose-built furniture and technical specifications.

A station might incorporate a durable sintered-stone worktop, a recessed ice well, speed rails for frequently used bottles, an under-counter glass hanger or a mobile console mounted on castors.

Some specialised systems also integrate dedicated pumps or other equipment to reduce repetitive preparation time.

These features are not simply technical details. They influence the rhythm of service, the bartender’s movement and the consistency of the guest experience.

### Ergonomics is part of the design language

A well-designed station makes complex work feel effortless.

The bartender should not need to cross the entire workspace to reach a frequently used ingredient. Glassware should be accessible without awkward movement. Ice, tools, bottles and waste should each have a logical place.

When the station works well, the guest may never notice the decisions behind it. They simply experience a faster, more fluid and more confident service.

This is an important principle of hospitality design: **operational efficiency can be invisible to the guest while remaining essential to the atmosphere.**

The station must also support the visual identity of the venue. Its materials, finishes, proportions and details should feel connected to the surrounding interior rather than appearing as an unrelated piece of industrial equipment.

The objective is to design one object that performs two roles: it supports the team and contributes to the experience of the room.

## 03 — FROM AN IDEA TO A BUILDABLE OBJECT

### Why a beautiful visualisation is not enough

A concept drawing can communicate the appearance of an object, but it does not automatically explain how to manufacture it.

A piece of custom furniture needs dimensions, materials, construction details and an understanding of how it will be assembled and installed. Equipment must fit its intended location and work with the requirements of the venue.

This is where conceptual design meets technical development.

The process typically moves through several stages:

**01. Concept direction**

Define the object’s purpose, visual identity and relationship to the venue. Establish what makes it distinctive and which requirements cannot be compromised.

**02. Sketching and proportion**

Explore the shape, layout, scale and relationship between components. At this stage, the goal is to understand the object before committing to detailed construction.

**03. Dimensions and materials**

Specify the principal measurements, surfaces, finishes and materials. For working furniture, consider durability, cleaning, moisture, heat, weight and the conditions of daily use.

**04. Three-dimensional visualisation**

Create a 3D representation to understand the object from different angles and assess how it fits within the room. This helps communicate the design to clients, fabricators and other members of the project team.

**05. Technical drawings**

Translate the visual concept into information that a workshop can use. Depending on the object, this may include plans, elevations, sections, dimensions, joinery details, component layouts and installation requirements.

**06. Prototype and testing**

Test the object at an appropriate scale or build a working prototype. Check proportions, comfort, reach, stability, durability and the practical details that may not be obvious in a rendering.

**07. Production and installation**

Confirm the final specification, manufacturing method, lead time, cost and installation requirements before committing to production.

Not every object needs a full technical drawing package. A printed menu card and a custom-built bar station require very different levels of development. The process should be proportionate to the complexity, cost and risk of the object.

The essential principle is that the design must move beyond appearance and become a clear, buildable proposal.

## 04 — THE THREE QUESTIONS BEHIND EVERY CONCEPT OBJECT

When evaluating a physical object for a hospitality venue, three questions help establish its role.

### What does the guest touch first?

This is the first physical point of contact between the concept and the guest.

It might be a door handle, a menu, a glass, a cup, a chair or a piece of tableware. These objects can establish expectations through their weight, temperature, texture, proportions and finish.

The first touch does not need to be spectacular. It needs to feel consistent with the experience that follows.

If a venue promises careful craftsmanship, a poorly finished menu or uncomfortable glass may contradict that promise. If the concept is relaxed and informal, an excessively precious object may create unnecessary distance.

The physical interaction should support the intended relationship between the venue and its guests.

### What does the guest take away?

This question concerns both literal objects and lasting impressions.

A guest might leave with a cup, a package, a small printed keepsake or a product purchased from the venue. Even when nothing physical leaves the room, the guest carries a memory of the objects, rituals and atmosphere they encountered.

A takeaway object should therefore express something meaningful about the concept. It should not feel like generic merchandise with a logo added at the end.

Consider its usefulness, desirability, production cost and connection to the venue’s identity. A simple, well-executed object can be more effective than an elaborate item that guests have no reason to keep.

### What does the team work behind?

The third question shifts attention from the guest-facing experience to the operational environment.

The bar station, shelving, storage, service trolley, preparation counter and equipment layout determine how the team performs its work.

These objects should be designed around actual tasks rather than assumptions about how a venue operates.

Consider the sequence of preparation, the distance between tools and ingredients, the number of people working simultaneously, cleaning requirements and the movement of products through the space.

A beautiful workspace that slows down service is not a successful design. Neither is an efficient station that creates a visual or practical conflict with the concept.

The strongest solutions reconcile both requirements.

## 05 — MATERIALS, DURABILITY & THE REALITY OF USE

A concept object must survive contact with everyday hospitality operations.

Menus are handled repeatedly. Cups are washed or disposed of. Coasters absorb moisture. Furniture is moved, cleaned and exposed to wear. Bar stations encounter spills, heat, heavy equipment and constant movement.

Material selection must therefore consider more than colour and appearance.

**Paper and print** offer flexibility and relatively straightforward replacement. Paper weight, coating, texture and print quality influence both the appearance and durability of a menu.

**Wood** can introduce warmth and tactility, but its suitability depends on the finish, construction, moisture exposure and cleaning requirements.

**Metal** can communicate precision, permanence or industrial character. Its weight, temperature, finish and resistance to corrosion should be considered.

**Ceramics and glass** offer distinctive tactile and visual qualities but require attention to breakage, storage, washing and replacement.

**Stone and engineered surfaces** may work well for durable worktops, depending on the specific material, construction and installation requirements.

**Textiles and upholstery** influence comfort and atmosphere, but they must also suit the expected level of use, cleaning regime and maintenance budget.

There is no universally correct material. The right choice depends on the object’s role, the conditions it will encounter and the message it needs to communicate.

A concept becomes more credible when its materials are chosen for both expressive and practical reasons.

## 06 — PROTOTYPES, UNIT COST & THE BUSINESS MODEL

A physical object is also a financial decision.

An item may look excellent in a presentation but become impractical when produced in the required quantity. A custom glass, printed menu or bespoke furniture piece must be evaluated in terms of manufacturing, delivery, maintenance and replacement.

The relevant costs depend on the type of object, but may include:

* Design and development.
* Materials and manufacturing.
* Printing, finishing or customisation.
* Packaging and transport.
* Installation or assembly.
* Cleaning and maintenance.
* Breakage, wear and replacement.

It is useful to distinguish between **initial development cost** and **unit cost**.

Initial development may include design work, prototyping, tooling or the preparation of technical drawings. Unit cost refers to the cost of producing each individual object at a specified production quantity.

For example, a custom coaster may have a modest unit cost but require an initial investment in design and printing setup. A bespoke bar station may require significant design and fabrication work, with its value assessed through long-term durability, operational benefits and the demands of the venue.

Production volume also matters. A material or manufacturing method that works for ten pieces may not be the most economical choice for several hundred.

A prototype helps test both the object and the assumptions behind its cost. It can reveal problems with proportions, usability, durability or manufacturing before a larger investment is made.

**A concept object is complete only when its design, production method, cost and intended use make sense together.**

## 07 — BUILDING A COHERENT OBJECT FAMILY

The strongest hospitality concepts rarely rely on one isolated object. They develop a family of objects that share a recognisable design language.

This does not mean that every object should look identical. Instead, the objects can share a consistent approach to materials, proportions, colours, graphics, detailing or construction.

Imagine a contemporary coffee concept built around the idea of daily rituals.

Its menu might use a compact typographic system. The takeaway cup could repeat a distinctive graphic element. Packaging could use the same paper palette and restrained language. Furniture might combine simple forms with durable natural materials.

The bar station could then extend the same logic through its proportions, surfaces and carefully organised layout.

Each object has a different purpose, but together they make the concept feel coherent.

The same approach works for cocktail bars, restaurants, bakeries, wine bars and hotels. The object family should grow from the central identity of the venue, not from a desire to decorate every surface.

Consistency gives the guest a sense that the entire experience has been considered.

## THE TAKEAWAY

A hospitality concept becomes real through the objects that guests and teams encounter every day.

The menu introduces the offer. Merchandise can extend the brand beyond the venue. Furniture shapes comfort and movement. The bar station supports the choreography of service. Materials, dimensions and manufacturing decisions determine whether these objects can perform their intended roles.

CooCoo Coffee illustrates how a simple product identity can extend across menus, cups and packaging. Purpose-built bar solutions demonstrate how furniture can unite visual design with operational performance.

Together, these examples reveal that concept development is not complete when the visual idea looks convincing. It is complete when the idea can be translated into objects that are recognisable, usable, producible and financially viable.

When designing any object for a venue, return to three questions:

* What does the guest touch first?
* What does the guest take away?
* What does the team work behind?

The answers help connect identity, guest experience and operational reality.

**Good concept design is not decoration applied to an object. It is the process of giving an object a clear purpose within a larger hospitality experience.**

# AUTOMATION, ERGONOMICS & THE HORECA CODE

**MODULE 04 · RESTAURANT & BAR CONCEPTS**

**LESSON 06 · 15 MIN**

*Technology and ergonomics should not simply make hospitality faster. They should free the team to be more present, attentive and human with the guest.*

## THE IDEA

### Look beyond the surface.

Exceptional hospitality often appears effortless. Drinks arrive at the right moment, service flows naturally, the team moves with confidence, and guests feel that they have the full attention of the people looking after them.

Behind that apparent simplicity lies a carefully designed system.

Preparation schedules, equipment placement, inventory management, recipes, batching, cleaning routines and service standards all influence what happens in front of the guest. When these systems work well, the team has more time and attention available for genuine hospitality. When they fail, even the most beautiful concept can become slow, inconsistent and stressful.

The objective of automation is not to remove people from hospitality. It is to remove unnecessary friction from their work.

**The machine should take care of repetition. The human should take care of the experience.**

This is the central relationship between automation, ergonomics and the operational philosophy explored through *The Horeca Code*: the visible experience of hospitality depends on the quality of the invisible system supporting it.

## 01 — THE HORECA CODE: BUILDING THE SYSTEM BEHIND THE EXPERIENCE

### Bek Narzi: From City Space Bar to operational discipline

Bek Narzi, associated with City Space Bar in Moscow and the London hospitality scene, including Pachamama, represents a useful starting point for understanding the relationship between creative ambition and operational structure. His work on *The Horeca Code* also points towards a broader principle: hospitality must be designed as both an experience and a business.

A venue may have an exceptional concept, a distinctive menu and an impressive interior, but these elements cannot compensate indefinitely for weak operational foundations.

The team needs to know how the venue works, what is expected of each role, how products are prepared, how stock is controlled and how performance is measured.

Operational discipline creates the conditions in which creative hospitality can be delivered consistently.

Several systems are particularly important.

**Standardised preparation**

Ingredients, garnishes, syrups, infusions and other components should be prepared according to documented specifications. This reduces unnecessary variation and allows the team to reproduce the intended result.

**Station ergonomics**

Tools, bottles, ice, glassware and equipment should be positioned according to the sequence of work. Frequently used items should be easy to reach, while less frequently used items should not obstruct the primary workspace.

**Inventory control**

The team needs to understand what enters the venue, what is consumed, what is wasted and what remains in stock. Reliable records support purchasing decisions, reduce losses and help maintain menu availability.

**Cost and profitability**

Every product has a cost structure. Ingredient usage, waste, labour, preparation time and selling price all influence the financial performance of the offer.

**Service standards**

A consistent experience depends on shared expectations. The team should understand how products are presented, how guests are welcomed, how problems are resolved and which details must never be compromised.

These systems are not separate from hospitality design. They make the concept sustainable in everyday operation.

## 02 — AUTOMATE REPETITION, PROTECT HUMAN ATTENTION

### Where should the machine take over?

Not every task in a hospitality venue needs to be performed manually. Some activities are repetitive, measurable and predictable. Others depend on judgement, emotional intelligence and the ability to respond to a particular guest.

The challenge is to distinguish between them.

Automation can be valuable when it improves consistency, reduces physical strain, saves time or makes a process more reliable. It becomes less useful when it removes an interaction that is central to the identity of the venue.

Consider a cocktail bar.

A batching system can prepare a repeatable base for a high-volume drink. A precise dispenser can control portion size. Inventory software can track stock and flag discrepancies. A temperature-controlled refrigerator can protect ingredients and improve consistency.

These systems can reduce repetitive work and help the team manage busy service periods.

But they cannot replace the bartender’s ability to recognise that a guest wants a recommendation, adjust an explanation to someone unfamiliar with the menu or respond warmly when an unexpected situation arises.

The objective is not to automate as much as possible. It is to automate the right things.

### A useful distinction

**Automate tasks that benefit from repetition and control:**

* Repeated measurements and portioning.
* Predictable preparation processes.
* Stock recording and routine reporting.
* Temperature monitoring and equipment alerts.
* Repetitive administrative tasks.
* Standardised components of high-volume production.

**Protect tasks that benefit from human judgement:**

* Welcoming guests and reading the mood of the room.
* Making recommendations based on individual preferences.
* Explaining the story behind a dish or drink.
* Responding to complaints or unexpected needs.
* Creating a sense of occasion.
* Recognising when a guest needs attention — or space.

The distinction is not absolute. Technology can support personalisation, and some manual processes may be important to a venue’s identity. The decision should always begin with the purpose of the interaction.

A useful question is: does this task become more valuable when a person performs it, or does it simply consume attention that could be used elsewhere?

## 03 — ERGONOMICS: DESIGNING MOVEMENT, NOT JUST FURNITURE

### The geometry of service

Ergonomics is the study of how work, equipment and environments can be designed around the people using them.

In a bar or restaurant, ergonomics affects speed, comfort, safety and consistency. It also influences the atmosphere because the way a team moves determines how service feels to the guest.

A poorly designed workstation forces staff to take unnecessary steps, reach awkwardly, cross paths or repeatedly interrupt their workflow. During a busy service, these small inefficiencies accumulate.

A well-designed station supports a natural sequence of movements.

For a bartender, this may mean placing ice, the primary bottles, mixing tools and glassware within a logical working area. For a chef, it may mean arranging ingredients, preparation surfaces and cooking equipment in the order they are used. For a server, it may mean planning access routes, tray storage and points of service so that movement remains fluid.

### Designing a bar station around workflow

Begin with the task rather than the furniture.

**Step 1: Map the sequence.**

Document the steps involved in preparing a representative drink or completing a common service task, from the first movement to the final handoff.

**Step 2: Identify repeated movements.**

Observe where staff reach, turn, walk, bend or wait. Pay particular attention to movements repeated dozens of times during a shift.

**Step 3: Position equipment logically.**

Place frequently used tools and ingredients close to their point of use. Keep less frequently used equipment accessible without allowing it to obstruct the primary work area.

**Step 4: Reduce unnecessary crossings.**

Consider where multiple team members work simultaneously. Separate conflicting routes where possible and ensure that doors, drawers and equipment do not create bottlenecks.

**Step 5: Test the layout in practice.**

A drawing can suggest an efficient arrangement, but a real workflow test reveals whether it works. Check reach, visibility, stability, cleaning access and the ability to work safely under pressure.

**Step 6: Refine and document.**

Once the arrangement works, record the layout and the intended location of equipment. Consistency helps new team members learn the station and reduces the chance that the workspace gradually becomes disorganised.

The goal is not to make every movement as fast as possible. It is to make necessary movements natural, safe and repeatable.

## 04 — SPEED WITHOUT LOSING THE CRAFT

### Artem Talalay and the “Cocktail Against the Clock” principle

Timed cocktail challenges illustrate how preparation, equipment placement and workflow can support speed without necessarily compromising the quality of the final drink.

When a bartender must produce several complex serves within a limited period, the result depends on more than hand speed. It depends on how much of the work has already been organised.

Recipes must be understood. Ingredients must be ready. Garnishes must be prepared. Glassware must be accessible. Tools must be positioned logically. The sequence of production must minimise unnecessary movement and waiting.

This is the difference between rushing and designing for speed.

A well-prepared bartender can work quickly because fewer decisions need to be made during service. The system has already resolved many of the practical questions.

### Speed rails and the logic of reach

A speed rail holds frequently used bottles within easy reach of the bartender. Its position, capacity and organisation should reflect the actual menu and the sequence in which ingredients are used.

If a bartender repeatedly reaches across the station or searches for a bottle, the problem may not be the person’s speed. It may be the layout.

The same principle applies to ice wells, tool storage, glass hangers, garnish trays and waste containers.

Equipment should be arranged around the work rather than forcing the work to adapt to an arbitrary arrangement.

### Batching as a tool for consistency

Batching involves preparing selected ingredients or components in advance so that service requires fewer individual steps.

Depending on the recipe and food-safety requirements, this may involve preparing a measured cocktail base, combining ingredients that remain stable together or producing a standardised component for use during service.

The benefits can include:

* Faster preparation during peak periods.
* More consistent proportions.
* Reduced repetitive measuring.
* Better control over preparation time.
* A more predictable workflow for the team.

However, batching must be designed carefully. Not every ingredient can be combined in advance without affecting freshness, carbonation, texture or flavour. Storage conditions, shelf life, labelling and food-safety procedures must be considered.

A process is not improved simply because it is faster. It is improved when it becomes faster **without compromising the intended result or creating new risks**.

## 05 — PREPARATION TECHNOLOGY AS A CREATIVE TOOL

### From clarification to controlled production

Modern food and beverage development uses a wide range of techniques to control texture, clarity, aroma, temperature and presentation.

Clarification can create a cleaner-looking liquid while changing its texture and perceived character. Dehydration can produce garnishes that are easier to store and handle. Centrifugation and rotary evaporation can support specialised ingredient processing and flavour development when used appropriately.

These techniques can expand what a kitchen or bar is able to create, but technology should always serve a defined purpose.

A piece of equipment is not valuable simply because it is sophisticated. It is valuable when it solves a problem, improves a product or makes a creative result repeatable.

Before investing in preparation technology, consider:

* What specific product or process will it improve?
* How often will it be used?
* Does the team have the training to operate it safely?
* What are the cleaning and maintenance requirements?
* How much preparation time or waste will it actually save?
* Is the improvement meaningful enough to justify the investment?

For some venues, advanced equipment may be central to the concept. For others, a small number of reliable tools and well-designed preparation routines will deliver greater value.

The right level of technology depends on the offer, the team, the volume of service and the business model.

## 06 — THE INVISIBLE MACHINE

### When operational systems become part of the atmosphere

A guest does not usually want to see every operational process behind a hospitality experience.

They may appreciate watching a bartender prepare a drink or a chef finish a dish, but they are less likely to welcome overflowing waste containers, frantic searches for equipment, staff collisions or constant interruptions.

The distinction is between **visible craft** and **visible friction**.

Visible craft is intentional. It allows guests to observe a meaningful part of the work. A bartender crushing fresh herbs, a chef finishing a plate or a server presenting a dish can reinforce the identity of the venue.

Visible friction is unplanned. It exposes the consequences of a poorly organised workspace, insufficient preparation or unclear responsibilities.

The objective is not to hide every process. It is to decide which processes belong in the guest experience and which should remain in the background.

This decision should be made during concept development.

An open kitchen, an interactive cocktail counter or a chef’s table may deliberately expose parts of the production process. In these settings, visibility becomes part of the concept, and the working environment must be designed to support it.

A more intimate dining room may require greater separation between guest-facing areas and operational activity.

Neither approach is inherently superior. What matters is that the visibility of work is intentional and consistent with the promise of the venue.

## 07 — INVENTORY, COST CONTROL & THE REALITY OF PROFITABILITY

### Operational precision protects creative ambition

A venue may develop an exceptional menu and still struggle if it cannot control purchasing, waste, portion sizes and product costs.

Inventory management helps the team understand the relationship between what is purchased, what is used, what is sold and what is lost.

For a bar, this may involve recording bottle quantities, monitoring the use of expensive ingredients, tracking prepared batches and reviewing discrepancies between expected and actual consumption.

For a restaurant, it may involve recipe costing, yield calculations, portion control, waste recording and purchasing based on demand.

These systems do not need to be unnecessarily complicated. They need to produce reliable information that the team can use.

### From recipe to financial performance

Every menu item should have a defined specification and a clear understanding of its ingredient costs.

That information supports decisions about pricing, portion sizes, purchasing and menu design. It also makes it easier to understand the financial effect of changing an ingredient or preparation method.

The check average — the average amount spent per guest or transaction, depending on how the business measures it — is another useful indicator. It can help the team understand purchasing patterns and assess how the offer performs.

However, increasing the average spend should not become an excuse for aggressive selling or unnecessary complexity. Recommendations should remain relevant to the guest, and commercial decisions should support the intended experience.

The strongest operation aligns three objectives:

* A product that delivers the promised quality.
* A service experience that feels natural and generous.
* A business model capable of sustaining both.

Cost control is not the opposite of creativity. When handled intelligently, it helps protect the resources needed to deliver creativity consistently.

## 08 — THE HUMAN / MACHINE BALANCE

The most effective hospitality operations are not necessarily the most automated. They are the ones that understand where technology adds value and where human presence matters most.

Consider the journey of a guest arriving for an evening cocktail.

Before arrival, a reservation system may manage bookings and communicate essential information.

During preparation, inventory software may help the team monitor stock, while measured batches and a logical workstation reduce repetitive tasks.

When the guest arrives, the host or bartender can focus on welcoming them, understanding their preferences and helping them navigate the menu.

During service, the team uses established recipes and equipment layouts to maintain consistency, while retaining the judgement to respond to individual needs.

At the end of the visit, a reliable payment system can make the transaction straightforward without replacing a genuine farewell.

Technology supports the journey, but the human moments give it meaning.

The same logic applies across hospitality: automate the background tasks that can be standardised, while preserving the interactions that create trust, comfort, discovery and emotional connection.

## THE TAKEAWAY

Technology, ergonomics and operational discipline are not separate from hospitality design. They form the infrastructure that allows the guest experience to work.

The principles associated with *The Horeca Code* highlight the importance of standards, inventory control, preparation and financial awareness. The speed-focused approach demonstrated in cocktail development shows how batching, equipment placement and workflow design can support both quality and efficiency.

Together, these ideas reveal that speed is rarely the result of movement alone. It is the result of preparation, organisation and well-designed systems.

A thoughtfully planned station reduces unnecessary steps. A reliable preparation process protects consistency. Clear inventory procedures support profitability. Appropriate automation frees the team from repetitive work.

Most importantly, these systems create space for the parts of hospitality that technology cannot fully reproduce: attention, judgement, warmth and the ability to make a guest feel genuinely welcome.

**The best operational system is not the one guests notice. It is the one that allows them to notice the hospitality.**

# LESSON 07 — AI AS A CREATIVE AND OPERATIONAL TOOL

*Editorial Lesson · 17 min*

## THE IDEA: LOOK BEYOND THE SURFACE

Artificial intelligence is becoming part of hospitality, from creative research and menu development to demand forecasting, inventory planning and preparation scheduling. For small teams, it can expand the range of possibilities without requiring more time or resources.

But efficiency is not the same as authorship. AI can generate hundreds of flavour combinations, visual references or menu concepts. It cannot independently determine which idea deserves to exist, what a venue should stand for, or how a guest should feel when they walk through the door.

The opportunity is to use AI as an extension of the creative and operational process—not as a substitute for human judgement. Let it accelerate research, expose alternatives and organise complexity. Then apply taste, experience and a clear point of view to decide what belongs in the final result.

The most important question is not *What can AI create?* It is *What can we do better when AI becomes part of our process?*

## 01 — AI AS A CREATIVE CATALYST

AI can help translate an abstract idea into a set of tangible possibilities. A few carefully written prompts can generate visual directions, suggest flavour relationships, propose ingredient matrices or explore how a menu might communicate a concept.

This is particularly useful during early-stage research and development, when the goal is to explore multiple directions before committing to one.

A team might use AI to:

* Generate visual references for a venue's atmosphere, materials and objects.
* Explore flavour combinations around a specific ingredient, season or cultural reference.
* Build a preliminary matrix of ingredients, techniques, textures and aromas.
* Develop alternative menu structures, naming directions or storytelling approaches.
* Turn an initial idea into a brief that can be tested by the bar or kitchen team.

However, generated options are hypotheses, not finished creative decisions. They may be technically plausible yet culturally superficial, visually generic or disconnected from the actual experience of hospitality.

The value lies in what the team does next: selecting, questioning, testing and refining.

## 02 — THE WORLD CLASS AI BRIEF: FROM IMAGE TO SENSORY EXPERIENCE

The Diageo World Class challenges provide a useful framework for considering how a digital stimulus can become a physical drink. In an AI-led creative brief, a generated image or visual concept can serve as the starting point for a sensory interpretation.

A visual might suggest a particular landscape, colour palette, atmosphere or emotional quality. The bartender's task is to translate those associations into something a guest can taste, smell, touch and experience.

The process might move through four stages:

**01. Interpret.** Identify the qualities behind the image rather than copying its appearance. Is it restrained or extravagant? Mineral or botanical? Warm, cold, nostalgic or futuristic?

**02. Translate.** Connect those qualities to ingredients, aroma, temperature, texture, colour, dilution and glassware.

**03. Prototype.** Prepare several versions and test how well they communicate the original idea. An attractive image does not guarantee a balanced or enjoyable drink.

**04. Edit.** Remove elements that exist only to impress. Retain the details that strengthen the sensory experience and make the concept understandable.

A drink inspired by an AI-generated image should not merely resemble a picture. It should express an interpretation of the idea behind it.

The final experience still depends on craft: the balance of the liquid, the quality of the ice, the weight of the glass and the way the drink is presented to the guest.

## 03 — SIMONE CAPORALE: EXPERIMENTATION WITH INTENTION

Simone Caporale's creative practice offers a useful lens through which to consider experimentation in contemporary drinks. Creative technology becomes valuable when it supports a clear idea and leads to a tangible experience.

AI can help a team explore an unfamiliar direction, organise research or produce a first draft of a creative brief. But the resulting drink must still be evaluated through professional judgement: Does it taste right? Does the technique serve the idea? Is the result repeatable? Does it belong within the identity of the venue?

This distinction is essential. A tool can generate novelty, but novelty alone is not a concept.

For hospitality teams, the practical principle is to separate **idea generation from creative approval**. AI can participate in the first stage. The responsibility for the second remains with the people who understand the ingredients, the guests and the standards of the business.

## 04 — RÉMY SAVAGE: THE IMPORTANCE OF A POINT OF VIEW

Rémy Savage's conceptual approach to bar design and menu development provides another important perspective: a strong creative identity requires more than a collection of attractive ideas.

A venue needs an underlying logic. Its references, language, drinks, materials and service should feel connected by a coherent point of view.

AI can help explore how a concept might look or sound, but it can also produce polished, interchangeable results. A sophisticated moodboard or persuasive paragraph does not necessarily reveal an original philosophy.

To use AI without losing authorship, ask three questions:

* **What is the idea?** Can the concept be expressed clearly without relying on decorative language?
* **Why does it matter?** What observation, cultural reference, place or human need makes it relevant?
* **What makes it ours?** Which decisions come from the team's experience, perspective and specific understanding of the venue?

Use the output as material to interrogate, not a direction to follow automatically.

A useful editing rule is simple: if a generated element could belong equally well to dozens of unrelated venues, it probably needs a more specific reason to exist.

## 05 — AI AS AN OPERATIONAL TOOL

The creative applications of AI are visible, but some of its most immediate value may be found in operational planning.

Hospitality businesses work with variable demand, perishable ingredients, changing team capacity and narrow service margins. AI-assisted analysis can help organise these moving parts, provided the underlying information is accurate and the results are checked.

### Demand forecasting

Historical sales, day of the week, seasonality, reservations and relevant local events can help inform expected demand. A forecasting tool may identify patterns that are difficult to spot manually.

These estimates can support purchasing, preparation quantities and staffing plans. They should remain forecasts rather than guarantees, particularly when data is incomplete or conditions change unexpectedly.

### Menu engineering and recipe development

AI can help structure recipe data, compare ingredient costs, organise flavour combinations and identify potential overlaps across a menu.

It can also help teams model how a change in portion size, ingredient price or selling price might affect contribution margin.

The calculations still need reliable inputs. A model cannot correct inaccurate supplier prices, inconsistent recipes or missing waste data simply by presenting the results confidently.

### Preparation and scheduling

AI-assisted planning can help estimate preparation requirements, organise production lists and coordinate tasks around expected service volume.

The objective is to reduce unnecessary administrative work and improve readiness—not to remove the team's ability to respond to changing circumstances.

### Research and documentation

Teams can use AI to summarise research, compare reference concepts, structure standard operating procedures and turn scattered notes into a clearer R&D brief.

Sensitive business information should only be entered into tools approved for that purpose, with access and data-handling rules understood by the team.

Across all these applications, the same principle applies: **automate the analysis where it is useful, but retain human responsibility for the decision.**

## 06 — THE R&D MATRIX: TURNING IDEAS INTO TESTABLE QUESTIONS

One practical use of AI is to make research and development more structured.

Instead of asking for a finished cocktail, begin with a specific creative question. For example: *How might a non-alcoholic drink express the atmosphere of a coastal landscape without relying on familiar tropical flavours?*

AI can help organise the exploration into a matrix:

* **Aroma:** saline, herbal, citrus, floral or mineral associations.
* **Texture:** crisp, silky, effervescent, viscous or dry.
* **Temperature:** chilled, ambient or deliberately contrasting.
* **Technique:** infusion, clarification, carbonation, fermentation or another suitable method.
* **Presentation:** glass shape, garnish, colour and service ritual.
* **Practicality:** ingredient availability, preparation time, stability, cost and repeatability.

The team can then select a limited number of combinations for physical testing.

This approach is more useful than asking AI to generate endless recipes. It creates a bridge between imagination and the realities of a working bar or kitchen.

Every proposed combination should be evaluated for flavour, safety, feasibility, cost and fit with the venue's concept. AI-generated recipes should never be assumed to be food-safe or technically validated without review.

## 07 — THE ETHICS CHARTER: WHERE AI SHOULD STOP

Using AI responsibly requires more than choosing the right software. It requires a shared understanding of where the technology is appropriate and where it should not replace human attention.

### Guest privacy

Guest data may include reservation details, preferences, allergies and personal information. Use only the information necessary for a legitimate purpose, follow applicable privacy requirements and avoid uploading identifiable guest information to unapproved systems.

Personalisation should make hospitality more considerate, not make guests feel monitored.

### Human judgement in service

AI may help identify patterns in reservations or service demand. It should not become the sole authority on how a guest should be treated.

A host noticing fatigue, discomfort or a special occasion is responding to a human situation that may not be fully represented in structured data. Hospitality depends on discretion, empathy and the ability to adapt.

### Team scheduling and fairness

Predictive tools can assist with staffing plans, but their recommendations need to be reviewed for fairness, labour requirements, team wellbeing and the realities of service.

A schedule that looks efficient in a spreadsheet may be unreasonable for the people expected to execute it.

### Creative authorship and transparency

Teams should be clear about how AI contributes to their work, especially when using generated imagery, text or concepts in public-facing materials.

Respect the rights associated with source material, verify references and avoid presenting generated work as evidence of research or experience that never took place.

### A practical decision rule

Before adopting an AI workflow, ask:

1. Does it solve a real creative or operational problem?
2. Is the data appropriate and handled responsibly?
3. Can the team verify the output?
4. Who remains accountable if the recommendation is wrong?
5. Does it preserve the quality of the guest and team experience?

If these questions cannot be answered clearly, the workflow needs further review before implementation.

## 08 — THE HUMAN–MACHINE BALANCE

AI is most useful when it reduces friction without making the hospitality experience feel automated in the wrong places.

Consider the guest journey. Demand forecasting may help the team prepare for a busy evening. Recipe analysis may reveal an opportunity to reduce waste. A structured brief may help a designer explore a new visual direction.

These are valuable applications because they improve the conditions in which people work.

The welcome, the explanation of a drink, the adjustment of a recommendation and the response to an unexpected situation require a different kind of attention. Technology may support these moments, but it should not determine every response.

The goal is not to make a venue feel technologically advanced. It is to make the venue more thoughtful, consistent and capable.

A successful AI workflow may be almost invisible to the guest. Its value appears in better preparation, fewer avoidable mistakes, more considered creative decisions and a team with greater capacity to focus on people.

## THE TAKEAWAY

AI can widen the creative field, accelerate research and make operational planning more informed. Its usefulness depends on the quality of the question, the reliability of the data and the judgement applied to the result.

Use it to generate possibilities, structure information and test assumptions. Do not confuse fluent output with original thinking, a forecast with certainty or automation with hospitality.

The strongest approach combines computational speed with human taste, operational knowledge and ethical responsibility.

## COURSE CONNECTION

This lesson introduces AI as a supporting layer within the hospitality concept developed throughout the course.

Your final venue concept and mockup should demonstrate not only a distinctive identity, but also a credible way of bringing it to life. AI can support the research behind the concept, help explore visual directions, structure menu development and inform operational decisions. The final choices must remain coherent with the venue's philosophy, audience, service model and practical constraints.

As you develop the complete project, consider where technology can strengthen the work behind the scenes, where human judgement must remain central, and how the two can support one another.

The objective is not to create an AI-designed venue. It is to create a well-authored hospitality concept in which every tool—including AI—has a clear purpose.

# LESSON 08 — THE NEXT TABLE & HIROYASU KAYAMA’S FARM-TO-GLASS APOTHECARY

*Editorial Lesson · 15 min*

## THE IDEA: LOOK BEYOND THE SURFACE

When every bar buys the same bottles from the same distributors, differentiation becomes difficult. A distinctive hospitality concept needs more than a recognisable aesthetic or an inventive menu. It needs a relationship with the ingredients, places, people and processes that give its offer meaning.

The next evolution of food and beverage is not simply about sourcing better products. It is about understanding the entire journey from raw material to finished experience: where ingredients grow, when they are harvested, how they are transformed, and why they belong in a particular venue.

Hiroyasu Kayama's Bar Benfiddich in Tokyo offers a compelling example of this philosophy. Its identity is rooted in botanical knowledge, house-made preparations, traditional techniques and direct conversation with guests. The bar operates less like a conventional cocktail menu and more like an apothecary, where ingredients and the person ordering the drink shape the final creation.

Other models extend the same principle in different directions. Tuju in São Paulo explores the relationship between cuisine and environmental cycles. Noma in Copenhagen helped bring foraging, fermentation and Nordic seasonality into the international conversation. Dave Arnold's work in beverage science demonstrates how experimentation can make unusual ideas precise and repeatable.

Together, these examples reveal a broader principle: **the strongest food and beverage concepts create a distinctive relationship between place, product, technique and hospitality.**

## 01 — HIROYASU KAYAMA: THE BAR AS AN APOTHECARY

<text>Bar Benfiddich, Tokyo</text>

<AsyncImageGroup query={["Bar Benfiddich Tokyo Hiroyasu Kayama antique bottles candlelit bar interior","Bar Benfiddich Hiroyasu Kayama herbs mortar pestle cocktail preparation"]} aspectRatio="5:4" layout="bento" />

At Bar Benfiddich, bartender and owner Hiroyasu Kayama has developed an approach to cocktails that places botanical knowledge and hands-on preparation at the centre of the experience.

His work is closely connected to the cultivation and transformation of ingredients, including botanicals grown on land in the Chichibu area of Saitama Prefecture. Wormwood, fennel, anise, juniper, chamomile, mint, plums and yuzu represent the kind of raw materials that can become part of a bar's own flavour vocabulary.

Through infusions, distillation and other preparations, familiar ingredients can be transformed into house-made spirits, bitters, liqueurs and modifiers. The result is a flavour identity built through knowledge and process rather than purchased entirely from a distributor.

This is the essential distinction between using an ingredient and developing a signature around it.

A bottle available to every competitor may be excellent, but the way a venue cultivates, processes, blends and serves its ingredients can become much harder to reproduce.

### From ingredient to identity

A farm-to-glass philosophy does not require every bar to own agricultural land. It requires the team to understand what makes its ingredients meaningful and how that meaning can be expressed in the final drink.

This might involve:

* Growing a small selection of herbs that are used regularly in signature serves.
* Developing a house cordial, tincture, infusion or seasonal shrub.
* Working directly with a nearby grower to select varieties that suit the menu.
* Preserving seasonal produce through fermentation, drying, infusion or another appropriate technique.
* Documenting the origin, preparation method, flavour profile and practical limitations of each house-made ingredient.

The objective is not to produce everything in-house. It is to identify the few preparations that have the greatest impact on the venue's identity.

A thoughtful house-made ingredient should contribute something meaningful to flavour, storytelling, guest experience or operational consistency.

## 02 — THE ZERO-MENU EXPERIENCE: HOSPITALITY AS CONSULTATION

<AsyncImage query="Bar Benfiddich Tokyo bartender preparing bespoke cocktail at counter antique glassware" aspectRatio="16:9" width="100%" />

Bar Benfiddich is also an example of how a venue can communicate its identity without relying on a conventional printed cocktail menu.

Instead of choosing from a fixed list, guests can engage in a conversation with the bartender. Preferences, mood, flavour associations and curiosity become the starting points for a personalised drink.

This changes the nature of ordering. The guest is not simply selecting a product; they are participating in the creation of an experience.

The bartender becomes an interpreter, translating an informal conversation into decisions about ingredients, balance, texture, aroma and presentation.

### What makes this approach work?

A bespoke service model depends on more than improvisation. It requires a deep understanding of ingredients, a reliable preparation system and the ability to read the guest.

A team needs to understand its flavour library, know which preparations work together, and maintain sufficient consistency to deliver a considered result without making the interaction feel mechanical.

The physical environment supports this relationship. Antique vessels, botanical references, tools and the visible process of preparation help communicate the character of the bar before a drink is served.

However, a zero-menu format is not automatically better than a printed menu. It can create uncertainty for guests who prefer clear choices, transparent prices or time to decide.

The lesson is to design the ordering experience around the concept and audience. A personalised conversation is most effective when it feels welcoming rather than intimidating.

**Design principle:** the menu can be a document, a conversation or a combination of both. What matters is how clearly it communicates the venue's identity and helps guests participate in the experience.

## 03 — TUJU: A MENU THAT RESPONDS TO ITS ENVIRONMENT

<text>São Paulo, Brazil</text>

<AsyncImageGroup query={["Tuju restaurant Sao Paulo contemporary seasonal tasting dish","Tuju restaurant Sao Paulo ingredient driven fine dining presentation"]} aspectRatio="5:4" layout="bento" />

Tuju provides a different perspective on the relationship between a restaurant and its environment. Its ingredient-led approach invites consideration of how weather, agricultural conditions and seasonal changes can influence the menu.

Rather than treating seasonality as a simple calendar of ingredients, a restaurant can observe the conditions that shape them: rainfall, drought, temperature, availability and variations in quality.

These conditions influence not only what can be sourced, but also how ingredients taste, how they should be prepared and which techniques are most appropriate.

For a hospitality concept, this creates an opportunity to develop a menu that changes for a reason.

A seasonal update might reflect:

* A shift in the ingredients available from local producers.
* A change in flavour intensity or texture across a growing season.
* A preservation technique that extends the life of a short-lived ingredient.
* A new preparation developed in response to a particular harvest.
* A change in the guest experience that reflects the time of year.

This kind of menu communicates an ongoing relationship with the world beyond the venue's walls.

### Seasonality as a design system

Seasonality should not be reduced to changing the garnish or renaming a cocktail every few months. It can influence the menu's structure, the preparation schedule, the visual language and the way the team tells its story.

At the same time, environmental responsiveness requires flexibility. A concept should be able to adapt when a harvest is smaller than expected or a key ingredient becomes unavailable.

The strongest seasonal systems have a clear identity but are not dependent on a single ingredient being available at all times.

For a smaller operation, this might mean maintaining a stable core menu while introducing a limited number of seasonal dishes or drinks. The result is both distinctive and manageable.

## 04 — NOMA: FORAGING, FERMENTATION AND THE RESPONSIBILITY OF LEADERSHIP

<text>Copenhagen, Denmark</text>

<AsyncImage query="Noma Copenhagen restaurant Nordic seasonal ingredients fermentation jars foraging ingredients" aspectRatio="16:9" width="100%" />

Noma, founded by René Redzepi, played a major role in bringing Nordic ingredients, foraging, fermentation and hyper-seasonal cooking to global attention.

Its broader influence demonstrates how a restaurant can build an identity through sustained investigation of its surroundings. Wild ingredients, regional traditions and preservation techniques become more than menu components: they form a framework for research and creative development.

For hospitality designers and operators, the important lesson is not to imitate a famous restaurant's dishes. It is to understand how research can become a repeatable creative practice.

A team might document local plants, work with knowledgeable growers, test preservation methods and develop a reference library of seasonal ingredients. Over time, this knowledge can inform dishes, drinks, service rituals and the overall narrative of the venue.

### Innovation must include accountability

The Noma case also highlights an essential distinction between creative influence and responsible leadership.

In 2026, public allegations from former employees prompted renewed scrutiny of the restaurant's working culture. Redzepi acknowledged harmful aspects of his past leadership, apologised and stepped away from the restaurant.

This context matters because a hospitality concept is not created by a single visionary in isolation. It depends on the people who research, prepare, serve, clean, maintain and sustain the operation.

A distinctive menu cannot compensate for an unhealthy working environment. Nor should a creative philosophy be used to justify harmful treatment of a team.

The responsibility of a leader is to create the conditions in which people can do ambitious work without sacrificing dignity, safety or fairness.

**Design principle:** a venue's values must be visible in both its guest experience and its working culture. Responsibility is part of the concept, not an operational detail to be addressed later.

## 05 — DAVE ARNOLD: MAKING EXPERIMENTATION REPEATABLE

<text>Liquid Intelligence</text>

<AsyncImage query="Dave Arnold Liquid Intelligence cocktail science laboratory equipment drinks preparation" aspectRatio="16:9" width="100%" />

A strong ingredient philosophy needs a complementary technical foundation. Dave Arnold's *Liquid Intelligence* explores the science behind cocktails, including temperature, dilution, carbonation and other variables that shape how a drink tastes and behaves.

This scientific perspective is particularly useful when a bar develops house-made preparations or unusual serving techniques.

An infusion might taste excellent in one test but become inconsistent when prepared in a larger batch. A drink may be perfectly balanced at one temperature and noticeably different as it warms. Carbonation, ice size and dilution can all influence the final result.

Controlled experimentation helps the team understand these variables rather than relying entirely on intuition.

### From creative hypothesis to service standard

A useful development process has four stages:

**01. Form a hypothesis.** Identify what the technique is expected to achieve. Is the goal greater aromatic intensity, a clearer liquid, a different texture or improved consistency?

**02. Test one variable at a time.** Where practical, change a single parameter—such as steeping time, temperature or dilution—so the team can understand its effect.

**03. Record the result.** Document quantities, timings, equipment, sensory observations and any relevant safety or storage requirements.

**04. Validate in service conditions.** Confirm that the preparation works at the intended scale, can be reproduced by the team and maintains quality throughout its planned use.

This process turns experimentation into operational knowledge.

It also prevents a common mistake: adopting a sophisticated technique simply because it looks impressive. Technology is useful when it solves a specific problem or creates a meaningful improvement in the guest experience.

## 06 — BUILDING A SIGNATURE THROUGH HOUSE-MADE PREPARATIONS

A distinctive F&B concept does not need dozens of proprietary ingredients. A small number of well-developed preparations can establish a recognisable flavour language across a menu.

Consider a hypothetical neighbourhood bar built around bitter herbs, orchard fruit and local seasonal produce. Its signature might emerge from three interconnected preparations:

* **A house botanical tincture** that introduces a consistent aromatic profile to several drinks.
* **A seasonal fruit cordial** that changes with availability while maintaining a defined acidity and sweetness.
* **A bitter aperitif base** developed to connect the menu's more herbal and fruit-driven serves.

Each preparation can serve multiple purposes without making every drink taste the same. The menu develops coherence through shared ingredients, while individual serves remain distinct.

The team must also consider practical constraints: yield, preparation time, ingredient cost, storage, shelf life, food safety, training and batch consistency.

A house-made ingredient is not automatically more sustainable or economical than a purchased product. The decision should be based on the value it creates and the resources it consumes.

### The house-made test

Before adding a preparation to the permanent menu, ask:

* Does it contribute a recognisable signature?
* Is the flavour meaningfully different from available commercial alternatives?
* Can the team reproduce it consistently?
* Is the preparation safe, stable and appropriate for the intended use?
* Does its cost and labour requirement make sense at the planned sales volume?
* Can the team explain why it belongs in the concept?

If the preparation does not strengthen the experience or the business, its complexity may not be justified.

## 07 — IVAN LYASHUK, VLADIMIR NIKOLAEV AND ARTENDER: CREATIVITY AS A SHARED PRACTICE

Creative development does not have to happen in isolation. Education, professional exchange and structured challenges can help bartenders test ideas, develop technical confidence and learn from their peers.

The work associated with Ivan Lyashuk and Vladimir Nikolaev through Artender provides a useful lens for considering how a creative community can support individual development.

For a venue, this principle can be translated into internal practices: tasting sessions, shared recipe documentation, ingredient workshops and regular discussions about what is working on the menu.

A signature should not exist only in the memory of its creator. It should become knowledge that the team can understand, question and reproduce.

This is especially important when the concept relies on specialised techniques or ingredients that require careful handling.

A collaborative approach makes the creative identity more resilient. It allows the team to maintain standards, contribute new ideas and develop a shared sense of ownership.

## 08 — FROM TERROIR TO THE GUEST EXPERIENCE

The journey from ingredient to service is a chain of decisions. Each stage should reinforce the same underlying idea.

<text>Origin → Transformation → Composition → Service → Memory</text>

**Origin:** Where does the ingredient come from, and why is that source relevant to the concept?

**Transformation:** Which techniques reveal, preserve or change its character?

**Composition:** How does it work with other ingredients, and what role does it play in the menu?

**Service:** How is the result presented, explained and experienced by the guest?

**Memory:** What makes the experience distinctive enough to be remembered?

This sequence can guide both a small bar and a larger restaurant. It helps teams identify where the concept is strongest and where the story becomes disconnected from the actual product.

If a venue promotes local sourcing but cannot explain its ingredients, the narrative remains superficial. If a house-made preparation is technically impressive but contributes nothing to flavour or service, the process may be unnecessary.

The aim is coherence. The ingredient, the technique, the menu, the team and the guest experience should feel like parts of the same idea.

## THE TAKEAWAY

The future of food and beverage belongs to operators who understand their ingredients and take responsibility for how those ingredients become an experience.

Hiroyasu Kayama demonstrates the creative potential of botanical knowledge and house-made preparations. Tuju illustrates how environmental conditions can inform a menu. Noma's influence highlights the value of research and preservation, while its leadership controversy reinforces the need for accountability. Dave Arnold shows how scientific thinking can make creative experimentation more precise and repeatable.

These examples point toward a model of hospitality built on three foundations:

* **Provenance:** understand the ingredients and relationships behind the offer.
* **Process:** develop techniques that express the concept and can be reproduced reliably.
* **Responsibility:** build an experience that respects guests, producers and the people who deliver it.

The goal is not to make everything from scratch. It is to know which elements deserve your own signature—and to build the knowledge and systems required to sustain it.

## COURSE CONNECTION

This lesson develops the food and beverage identity of your final hospitality concept.

The concept should now begin to move beyond its visual direction and into the substance of what the venue serves. Consider how its location, audience and philosophy influence ingredient selection, menu structure, signature preparations, pricing and service style.

Your final venue concept and mockup should communicate the relationship between product and place. This might appear through a small house-made ingredient library, a seasonal menu structure, a distinctive preparation ritual, a considered bar or kitchen layout, or a clear explanation of how the menu will evolve.

The ambition is not to imitate a farm-to-glass apothecary or a world-famous restaurant. It is to identify the elements that make your own concept distinctive, credible and operationally achievable.

**A strong hospitality concept does not merely tell a story about its ingredients. It builds the systems, skills and relationships that allow the story to be tasted.**


# LESSON 09 — FROM IDEA TO OPERATING MODEL & TALENT ECOSYSTEM

*Editorial Lesson · 18 min*

## THE IDEA: BUILD A BUSINESS THAT CAN DELIVER ITS PROMISE

A hospitality concept begins with an idea, but it becomes a business only when that idea can be delivered consistently, by a team, within real financial constraints.

The atmosphere, menu, service rituals and visual identity may attract the first guests. What determines whether they return is the system behind the experience: how the team works, how the business earns money, how standards are maintained and how people develop over time.

The most successful hospitality founders build more than rooms and brands. They create operating models that can sustain their creative ambitions—and environments in which talented people can learn, contribute and eventually lead.

The careers of Bek Narzi, Igor Zernov and Boris Zarkov offer three complementary perspectives on this process. Narzi represents the entrepreneurial discipline behind hospitality operations. Zernov demonstrates how a small, distinctive venue can become the foundation of a wider creative ecosystem. Zarkov illustrates the possibilities of developing multiple concepts under a broader group identity.

Together, these models reveal a central principle: **a hospitality business becomes scalable when its economics, culture and creative vision reinforce one another.**

## 01 — BEK NARZI: THE ENTREPRENEURIAL FOUNDATIONS

<text>City Space Bar · The Horeca Code</text>

<AsyncImage query="City Space Bar Moscow panoramic cocktail bar interior skyline" aspectRatio="16:9" width="100%" />

Bek Narzi's work in hospitality, including his association with City Space Bar and his educational approach through *The Horeca Code*, provides a framework for understanding the business behind the experience.

A venue can have an original concept and exceptional creative direction while remaining financially fragile. Rent, labour, product costs, maintenance, marketing and fluctuating demand continue regardless of how compelling the brand appears.

The founder's responsibility is to connect creative ambition with operational reality.

This requires understanding the business at several levels:

* **Financial literacy:** knowing how revenue, costs, margins and cash flow interact.
* **Guest psychology:** understanding why people choose the venue, what they value and what encourages them to return.
* **Sales and service training:** helping the team communicate the offer and deliver a consistent experience.
* **Operational discipline:** establishing clear standards for preparation, service, stock control and closing procedures.
* **Public relations and storytelling:** communicating the concept in a way that earns attention without making promises the business cannot fulfil.

These are not separate management functions. They form a connected system.

A compelling story may attract guests, but the product and service must justify the promise. Strong service may increase loyalty, but the business still needs a viable cost structure. Efficient operations may protect margins, but they should not strip away the qualities that make the venue distinctive.

### The team as a high-performance unit

A useful way to think about a hospitality team is as a group of specialists working toward a shared standard.

Like a high-performance sports team, it needs clear roles, preparation, feedback and accountability. Talent matters, but talent alone does not create consistency.

The founder must define what good performance looks like, make training part of the routine and give people the conditions to improve.

Discipline should not mean rigid control for its own sake. It should reduce confusion, protect quality and allow individuals to use their judgement when circumstances change.

The goal is a team that understands not only what to do, but why it matters.

## 02 — UNIT ECONOMICS: MAKE THE CONCEPT FINANCIALLY LEGIBLE

Before committing to a venue, the founder needs to understand the relationship between capacity, demand, pricing and operating costs.

Unit economics translates the creative concept into a set of measurable assumptions. It helps answer a simple question: can the business generate enough contribution from its guests to support its operating model?

Several variables are especially important.

### Capacity and covers

Capacity describes how many guests the venue can accommodate at one time. Covers describe the number of guests served over a period.

A 30-seat bar does not necessarily serve only 30 guests per evening. Depending on the service format, average visit duration and demand, seats may be occupied by multiple groups over the course of service.

However, theoretical capacity is not the same as actual demand. A model should distinguish the maximum number of guests a venue could serve from the number it realistically expects to serve.

### Average check

The average check is the average amount spent per guest or transaction, depending on how the business defines and tracks it.

It influences revenue directly, but it must be considered alongside customer volume, pricing, product mix and the experience the venue promises.

A higher average check is not automatically better. Prices need to make sense for the audience, location, product quality and perceived value.

### Seat turns and length of stay

Seat turns describe how frequently a seat is occupied by a new guest during a defined period.

A fast-moving neighbourhood café and a destination cocktail bar may have very different operating rhythms. A long, considered visit might be central to one concept, while rapid table turnover may be essential to another.

The objective is not to maximise turns at any cost. It is to establish a service rhythm that supports both the financial model and the intended guest experience.

### Rent and labour

Rent should be evaluated in relation to realistic revenue expectations, not optimistic projections alone.

Labour costs must also reflect the service model. A venue built around individual consultations, complex preparation or elaborate tableside rituals may need more staff time per guest than a simpler operation.

Reducing labour without reconsidering the service promise can damage the experience. The more useful question is whether the team is organised effectively and whether each task contributes enough value to justify its time.

### Payback horizon and cash flow

A business can appear profitable on paper while struggling to meet its immediate financial obligations. Opening costs, deposits, equipment, stock, pre-opening payroll and early operating losses all affect the amount of cash required.

The founder needs to estimate how much capital is required to open, how much working capital is needed to sustain the first months, and under what conditions the investment could be recovered.

These projections are assumptions to test and revise, not guarantees.

### A simple operating model

A preliminary revenue model can begin with:

**Revenue = Covers × Average spend per guest**

For example, if a venue serves 60 guests per day and the average spend is €35, daily revenue would be €2,100 before considering taxes, discounts, refunds and the details of accounting treatment.

That figure alone says very little about profitability. The next step is to subtract the costs associated with delivering the experience, then account for fixed operating expenses, financing, taxes and other obligations.

The point is not to build a complicated spreadsheet immediately. It is to understand which assumptions drive the business and which need real-world validation.

## 03 — IGOR ZERNOV: FROM A SMALL VENUE TO A CREATIVE ECOSYSTEM

<text>El Copitas · #FollowTheRabbits</text>

<AsyncImageGroup query={["El Copitas bar Saint Petersburg intimate cocktail bar interior","El Copitas cocktail bar bartender tequila mezcal preparation"]} aspectRatio="5:4" layout="bento" />

El Copitas, associated with Igor Zernov, Artyom Peruk and Nikolay Kiselyov, offers a useful example of how a highly distinctive hospitality experience can become the foundation for broader entrepreneurial development.

The original concept was deliberately intimate, operating as a hidden, limited-service-hours bar rather than attempting to compete through scale or visibility alone. Its identity, hospitality and sense of discovery helped create a strong relationship with its audience.

The venue's international recognition, including its place among the World's 50 Best Bars, illustrates how a focused concept can gain influence well beyond its physical size.

The broader #FollowTheRabbits ecosystem subsequently connected different hospitality formats and initiatives, including El Copitas, Paloma Cantina, Tagliatella Caffe, Sangre Fresca, Bartenders FAQtory and Saint-Petersburg Cocktail Week.

The important lesson is not simply that one successful bar can open other businesses. It is that a strong initial concept can generate knowledge, relationships and an audience that support further experiments.

### Start small to learn faster

A limited-capital launch can help a founder test the assumptions behind a concept before committing to a more expensive format.

An intimate venue, a temporary event, a guest shift or a carefully designed pop-up can provide evidence about:

* Whether the intended audience understands and values the concept.
* Which products and experiences generate the strongest response.
* How much guests are willing to spend.
* Which operational processes are difficult to execute.
* What the team needs to learn before expanding.

A small format does not eliminate risk. It can, however, make some mistakes less expensive and provide an opportunity to refine the model before larger investments are made.

The key is to define what the experiment is intended to prove. A successful event may demonstrate demand for a particular menu, but it does not automatically prove that a permanent venue can cover its rent and payroll.

### Reinvest in what the business learns

As a concept develops, it accumulates more than revenue. It develops recipes, operating procedures, supplier relationships, service knowledge, brand recognition and a network of people.

These resources can support future projects.

A group might develop a new concept for a different audience, introduce a complementary food format or create educational events that connect professionals across the industry.

The strongest ecosystem is not a collection of unrelated businesses. Each part should have a clear purpose and a reason to exist alongside the others.

## 04 — FROM BRAND PORTFOLIO TO BUSINESS ECOSYSTEM

A multi-concept group has to solve a difficult design problem: how to share resources without making every venue feel the same.

The #FollowTheRabbits model provides a useful way to think about a network of distinct hospitality formats. Each concept can occupy its own cultural, culinary or commercial territory while benefiting from shared experience and relationships.

For example, different venues might serve different occasions:

* A destination cocktail bar built around discovery and specialist craft.
* A casual cantina designed for sociable, accessible drinking and food.
* A café format focused on daytime routines and neighbourhood familiarity.
* An educational platform that develops skills and connects professionals.
* An industry event that brings the wider community together.

The advantage is strategic variety. Each format can reach a different audience, explore a different price point and respond to a different occasion.

But a portfolio also introduces complexity. More venues require management capacity, financial oversight, recruitment, training and clear accountability. Shared resources only create value when they improve the business without weakening individual identities.

### What should be shared—and what should remain distinct?

**Potentially shared:** financial reporting, recruitment infrastructure, training resources, purchasing knowledge, selected suppliers, brand governance and administrative systems.

**Usually concept-specific:** guest experience, service rituals, menu identity, visual language, atmosphere, pricing strategy and the particular reason a guest chooses that venue.

These are guiding principles rather than fixed rules. The correct balance depends on the scale and structure of the group.

The strategic objective is to create economies of knowledge without creating sameness.

## 05 — BORIS ZARKOV: DISTINCT CONCEPTS, CONNECTED AMBITION

<text>White Rabbit Family · Krasota · IKRA</text>

<AsyncImageGroup query={["White Rabbit Moscow restaurant interior contemporary dining","Krasota Moscow immersive gastronomic theatre dining projection experience"]} aspectRatio="5:4" layout="bento" />

Boris Zarkov's work across White Rabbit Family, Krasota Gastro-Theatre and IKRA offers another perspective on the relationship between individual concepts and a wider hospitality ecosystem.

A group can develop a portfolio in which each project has its own audience, atmosphere and proposition while contributing to a broader entrepreneurial vision.

This requires more than visual differentiation. Each concept needs a reason to exist, an operating model appropriate to its offer and a team capable of delivering its particular promise.

An immersive dining concept, for example, may depend on coordination between culinary production, technical systems, storytelling and service timing. A restaurant built around a more traditional dining format will have different operational priorities.

The group's role is to support these distinct requirements while providing a framework for investment, leadership, learning and long-term development.

### The value of a talent bench

A portfolio becomes more resilient when it develops people who can take on greater responsibility.

A team member might begin in service, deepen their product knowledge, learn to supervise a shift and eventually lead a venue or contribute to a new opening.

This progression requires deliberate investment in training, feedback and management development. It cannot depend entirely on informal learning or the hope that talented people will figure everything out themselves.

A strong group makes career development visible. It gives people a reason to grow within the organisation and creates a larger pool of experienced professionals who can help new projects succeed.

## 06 — BUILD A SCHOOL INSIDE THE BUSINESS

<AsyncImage query="bartender education workshop professional mixology training group at bar" aspectRatio="16:9" width="100%" />

Training is often treated as a cost that must be minimised. In a hospitality business, it is also a way to protect quality, develop future leaders and retain organisational knowledge.

The educational logic associated with City Space Bar and the Bartenders FAQtory initiative highlights the importance of learning environments within the industry.

A venue does not need to establish a formal academy to adopt this principle. It can create a structured internal programme that helps people build the skills required by the concept.

### A practical talent-development system

**01. Define the standards.** Document what good service, preparation, communication and operational discipline look like.

**02. Teach the reasoning.** Explain why a procedure exists, not just how to follow it. Understanding helps staff make better decisions when conditions change.

**03. Practise in realistic conditions.** Use tastings, mock service, role-play and supervised repetition to develop confidence.

**04. Give feedback consistently.** Make feedback specific, timely and connected to observable behaviour.

**05. Create progression.** Define what someone needs to demonstrate before taking on more responsibility.

**06. Capture knowledge.** Record recipes, procedures, lessons from difficult services and improvements proposed by the team.

This creates a learning loop: standards inform training, training improves execution, and experience helps refine the standards.

### Culture is part of the operating model

An educational culture requires more than technical instruction. People need to be able to ask questions, raise concerns and admit mistakes without being humiliated.

Accountability remains essential, particularly where safety, money and service quality are involved. But accountability works best when expectations are clear and people receive the support needed to meet them.

A venue that wants consistent excellence must invest in the people responsible for producing it.

## 07 — THE 90-DAY LAUNCH: TURNING A PLAN INTO EVIDENCE

A launch plan should connect the concept to a sequence of decisions that can be tested and improved.

The first 90 days are not simply a marketing countdown. They are an opportunity to validate the operating model, develop the team and learn how the business behaves under real conditions.

A useful framework divides the period into three phases.

### Days 1–30: Establish the foundations

Focus on confirming the business assumptions and preparing the operation.

* Finalise the core menu, recipes and pricing logic.
* Confirm suppliers, stock procedures and production requirements.
* Define team roles, training standards and service expectations.
* Test the guest journey from arrival through payment and departure.
* Prepare financial reporting and a simple dashboard of key indicators.

### Days 31–60: Test and refine

Use controlled service periods, previews or selected events to identify gaps.

* Observe where guests hesitate, wait or need more explanation.
* Compare actual product usage and labour requirements with the model.
* Collect structured feedback from guests and the team.
* Identify bottlenecks in preparation, service and payment.
* Adjust the menu, workflow and staffing assumptions where evidence supports a change.

### Days 61–90: Build consistency

Focus on turning lessons into repeatable performance.

* Review sales, average spend, product margins and labour costs.
* Refine training and document improved procedures.
* Identify the most effective marketing and community partnerships.
* Establish a regular rhythm for team feedback and performance review.
* Set the next development priorities based on actual results.

This framework should be adapted to the project's size, funding, opening schedule and local requirements. A permanent restaurant opening involves different risks from a pop-up or a small cocktail bar.

The principle is to treat launch as a learning process rather than assuming the original plan will be correct in every detail.

## 08 — THE METRICS THAT CONNECT CULTURE AND COMMERCE

A business needs a limited set of indicators that reveal whether the concept is working financially and operationally.

Useful measures may include:

| Indicator                             | What it helps reveal                                         |
| ------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Covers and occupancy                  | How effectively the venue uses its capacity                  |
| Average spend per guest               | Whether pricing and product mix align with guest behaviour   |
| Product cost percentage               | How much revenue is consumed by ingredients and beverages    |
| Labour cost percentage                | How staffing costs relate to revenue                         |
| Contribution margin                   | How much remains after relevant variable costs               |
| Waste and stock variance              | Whether purchasing, preparation and inventory are controlled |
| Repeat visits and guest feedback      | Whether the experience encourages loyalty                    |
| Staff retention and training progress | Whether the business supports a sustainable team             |

These indicators should be interpreted together. A lower labour percentage, for example, may look positive while hiding understaffing, excessive waiting or declining service quality.

Similarly, a high average spend does not necessarily indicate a healthy business if guest volume is too low or the cost of delivering the experience is excessive.

The purpose of measurement is not to turn hospitality into a spreadsheet. It is to give the team enough information to make better decisions.

Financial performance, guest experience and team wellbeing should be considered as connected parts of the same operating model.

## THE TAKEAWAY

A hospitality business is a promise delivered repeatedly by people working within a financial system.

Bek Narzi's entrepreneurial framework emphasises operational discipline, commercial literacy and clear standards. Igor Zernov and #FollowTheRabbits demonstrate how a focused concept can develop into a broader ecosystem of venues, education and community. Boris Zarkov's portfolio offers a perspective on maintaining distinct concepts within a wider group.

The lessons converge around three priorities:

* **Build a viable model:** understand capacity, pricing, costs, cash flow and the assumptions behind the business.
* **Build a distinctive ecosystem:** let each concept serve a clear purpose while using shared resources intelligently.
* **Build people, not just operations:** make training, feedback, career progression and leadership development part of the business from the beginning.

A great venue can be built around a founder's vision. A sustainable hospitality business requires a team that can understand, deliver and eventually develop that vision together.

This lesson connects the creative concept developed throughout the course with the operating model required to sustain it.

The concept should communicate how guests move through the space, how the team delivers the experience, which operational resources are required and how the venue can remain financially sustainable.

It should also express the kind of working culture the business intends to create. Consider how training, leadership, shared knowledge and opportunities for development will support the quality of the guest experience over time.

The objective is not to design a venue that works only when its founder is present. It is to create a coherent hospitality model in which the space, product, economics and people reinforce one another.

**The room is the visible expression of the idea. The operating model and the team are what allow that idea to live beyond opening day.**



# LESSON 10 — SOUL BEFORE BUDGET: JOI ESPRESSO BAR & DENIS BOBKOV’S SALVAGE WORLDS

*Editorial Lesson · 16 min*

## THE IDEA: CHARACTER CANNOT BE ORDERED FROM A CATALOGUE

A venue does not need a large budget to feel alive. It needs a point of view, a coherent visual language and the patience to find objects that express it.

Some of the most compelling hospitality interiors are assembled rather than built from scratch. Antique dishes, vintage furniture, reclaimed timber, old mirrors, tarnished brass, hand-painted signs and second-hand equipment can create a sense of history that is difficult to reproduce with new, mass-produced objects.

The difference is not simply that second-hand objects cost less. It is that each object carries its own material qualities, marks of use and visual associations. When selected with intention, these details create a room that feels discovered rather than decorated.

Joi Espresso Bar, developed by course author Egor Tarasenko, offers a model of building a venue through street finds and flea-market discoveries. Denis Bobkov's work across Pub Life Group venues, including Black Swan and Bambule, provides a complementary example of using salvaged objects and antique materials to construct distinctive environments.

Both approaches begin with the same principle: **define the world you want to create before deciding what you need to buy.**

## 01 — JOI ESPRESSO BAR: A WORLD BUILT THROUGH DISCOVERY

<AsyncImageGroup query={["Joi Espresso Bar Egor Tarasenko interior vintage eclectic cafe","small independent espresso bar vintage furniture antique tableware eclectic interior"]} aspectRatio="5:4" layout="bento" />

Joi Espresso Bar demonstrates how a venue can develop character without relying on an expensive, unified fit-out.

Instead of purchasing an entire interior from a single supplier, the process begins with observation and collection. Furniture, tableware, lighting, decorative objects and functional equipment are sourced individually, often from places that would not normally be considered part of a conventional design procurement process.

The result can feel personal because the objects are not interchangeable. A worn wooden chair, an old porcelain cup or a small brass lamp brings a particular shape, surface and history into the room.

But collecting interesting things is not enough.

A flea market contains thousands of objects that may be attractive on their own but incoherent when placed together. The designer's role is to identify the pieces that belong to the same world.

### One point of view, many different objects

A second-hand interior needs a strong editorial filter.

That filter might be a particular period, a material palette, a cultural reference, a mood or a contrast between two visual languages. The objects can vary considerably as long as they contribute to the same underlying idea.

For example, a neighbourhood espresso bar might combine dark timber, old porcelain, small framed prints and utilitarian metal fixtures. The pieces need not match, but their proportions, textures and relationship to the space should feel deliberate.

The same logic applies to a more theatrical bar built around dark wood, aged brass, candlelight and antique mirrors.

In both cases, the identity comes from the selection process—not from the price or age of any individual object.

## 02 — DENIS BOBKOV: SALVAGE AS AN ART FORM

<text>Pub Life Group · Black Swan · Bambule · Abbey Players · The Bix</text>

<AsyncImageGroup query={["Black Swan Pub Shop Moscow vintage pub interior antique wood brass candlelight","Bambule Moscow bar restaurant eclectic antique interior vintage furniture","dark European pub interior antique wood paneling candlelight vintage mirrors"]} aspectRatio="5:4" layout="bento" />

Denis Bobkov's work with Pub Life Group offers another perspective on the use of salvage and antique objects in hospitality design.

The approach treats the interior as an assembled environment rather than a finished product purchased from a catalogue. Old doors, reclaimed wood, antique tableware, brass details, vintage posters and candleholders can become the components of a distinctive atmosphere.

These objects do more than fill a room. They influence how the room is read.

A substantial old door may create the feeling of entering a hidden space. Dark wood panelling can suggest permanence and intimacy. An assortment of aged brass and worn furniture can give a bar the impression of having evolved over many years, even when the venue itself is relatively new.

### History without imitation

There is a difference between using historical objects and reproducing a historical style.

A room filled with antique-looking furniture may still feel artificial if every object has been selected to communicate the same obvious reference. Genuine salvage introduces variation: differences in finish, proportion, wear and craftsmanship.

The challenge is to use that variation without allowing the space to become visually chaotic.

Bobkov's approach is useful because it treats sourcing as part of the creative process. The search for an object is not a final decorative step after the design has been completed. It can influence the design itself.

A found door may inspire a partition. A vintage cabinet may become a back-bar feature. An unusual table may determine the scale of a seating area.

The design develops through an exchange between the original idea and the things discovered along the way.

## 03 — WRITE THE FEELING BEFORE YOU GO HUNTING

Before searching flea markets, auctions or salvage yards, define the atmosphere you want to create.

This does not require a long manifesto. A few precise sentences can provide a useful framework.

Consider the difference between these two briefs:

*“We want a vintage, eclectic café with interesting furniture.”*

And:

*“We want a compact neighbourhood espresso bar that feels collected over time: tactile, slightly imperfect, intimate and familiar, with objects that reward a closer look without making the space feel precious.”*

The second brief provides a much stronger basis for making decisions. It establishes not only a visual direction, but also the relationship between objects and the intended guest experience.

Use five questions to sharpen the brief:

* **Atmosphere:** What should a guest feel upon entering?
* **Material:** Which surfaces should dominate—wood, metal, stone, ceramic, glass or fabric?
* **Time:** Should the room feel contemporary, inherited, industrial, domestic or deliberately timeless?
* **Density:** Should objects be sparse and carefully isolated, or layered and abundant?
* **Function:** Which objects must work hard, and which exist primarily to communicate character?

These answers become the criteria against which potential finds are evaluated.

A beautiful object that contradicts the concept is not a bargain. It is an unnecessary purchase.

## 04 — THE SALVAGE MAP: WHERE TO FIND THE RIGHT OBJECTS

<AsyncImageGroup query={["European flea market antique porcelain brass candlesticks vintage objects","architectural salvage yard reclaimed wooden doors timber","vintage furniture warehouse second hand chairs tables","antique shop vintage glassware silver tableware"]} aspectRatio="5:4" layout="bento" />

A distinctive interior can emerge from a wide range of sources. The important thing is to match the source to the type of object you need.

### Flea markets and antique stalls

Best for small objects that help establish the atmosphere: porcelain, glassware, candlesticks, framed prints, mirrors, trays, vases and decorative pieces.

These sources are particularly useful when you want variation rather than a uniform collection.

### Second-hand furniture shops and online resale

Useful for chairs, tables, shelving, cabinets, lamps and other functional pieces. They can provide access to established furniture designs at a fraction of the price of new equivalents.

Dimensions, condition and transportation costs should be checked before committing to a purchase.

### Architectural salvage yards

A strong source for doors, timber, handles, stone offcuts, metal fittings, panelling and other architectural elements.

These materials may become structural or semi-structural features of the venue. Their installation may require professional assessment, especially when they affect circulation, fire safety, accessibility or building performance.

### Auctions and business liquidations

Potentially valuable for larger furniture collections, commercial equipment, shelving and batches of tableware.

The apparent purchase price should be evaluated alongside buyer's fees, collection requirements, repairs and the time needed to prepare the items for use.

### The street and demolition projects

Unexpected finds can inspire creative solutions, but availability does not establish ownership or permission to take an object.

Only collect materials when you have the legal right to do so. Items recovered from demolition or construction sites may also require checks for contamination, structural condition or unsuitable coatings.

### Local makers and repair specialists

Not every element needs to be antique. A new shelf, custom cushion or simple metal bracket can help an old object function within a contemporary venue.

The strongest interiors often combine found objects with carefully made additions that bring them together.

## 05 — PATINA, REPAIR AND THE VALUE OF IMPERFECTION

<AsyncImageGroup query={["worn antique wooden table patina close up","aged brass candlestick patina vintage detail","antique porcelain cups irregular vintage collection"]} aspectRatio="5:4" layout="carousel" />

Patina is the visible evidence of an object's age and use: softened edges, changes in colour, small surface variations and the gradual character that develops through time.

In hospitality design, these qualities can make a space feel less manufactured. They also introduce a tactile contrast to polished counters, modern equipment and newly finished surfaces.

However, **patina is not the same as damage**.

A scratched table may be perfectly serviceable. A loose chair joint, unstable shelf or cracked drinking glass may create a genuine risk.

The designer must distinguish between imperfections that contribute to the atmosphere and defects that undermine safety, hygiene or durability.

### Repair before replacing

Before discarding an object, consider whether it can be repaired, refinished, reupholstered or adapted.

A wooden table may need sanding and resealing. A chair may need new upholstery or strengthened joints. A vintage cabinet may become suitable for storage after the addition of internal shelving.

Repair can preserve the character of an object while extending its useful life.

Yet restoration should be proportionate. A heavily damaged item that requires extensive specialist work may cost more than a suitable alternative once labour and transport are included.

The aim is not to save every object. It is to make informed decisions about which pieces deserve to remain in use.

## 06 — SECOND-HAND DOES NOT MEAN SECOND-RATE

A low purchase price is only one part of an object's cost.

A useful comparison includes:

**Total acquisition cost = Purchase price + Transport + Repair + Adaptation + Installation**

For some items, ongoing maintenance and replacement risk also matter.

An inexpensive vintage chair may require extensive upholstery work. A second-hand espresso machine may need specialist servicing, new components and a reliable maintenance plan. A reclaimed timber counter may require treatment and fabrication before it can be installed.

By contrast, a well-maintained used table may be ready for service immediately and offer better value than a new equivalent.

### Evaluate objects according to their role

**Atmosphere objects:** Small decorative pieces, framed prints and candlesticks can tolerate greater variation, provided they remain safe and appropriate for the environment.

**Guest-contact objects:** Chairs, tables, cups, plates and glasses need to meet requirements for stability, cleanliness, comfort and repeated use.

**Operational equipment:** Espresso machines, refrigeration, dishwashing equipment and other technical systems need to meet performance, hygiene, maintenance and safety requirements.

**Architectural elements:** Doors, partitions, counters and built-in components must be assessed for their intended use, durability, accessibility and compliance with relevant building requirements.

The more critical an object is to safe and reliable operation, the less appropriate it is to base the decision on appearance alone.

## 07 — DESIGN AROUND WHAT YOU FIND, WITHOUT LOSING CONTROL

One of the most interesting aspects of salvage-led design is that the object can influence the architecture.

A vintage cabinet might determine the configuration of a retail display. A reclaimed door might become a visual anchor. A collection of old chairs may suggest a more informal seating arrangement than the original plan.

This flexibility can produce a richer result, but it also introduces risk. If every new discovery changes the layout, budget and design direction can drift indefinitely.

A practical approach is to distinguish between fixed requirements and adaptable elements.

### Fix the essentials

These should be resolved early:

* Guest circulation and accessibility.
* Staff workflow and operational clearances.
* Required utilities and equipment connections.
* Safety, hygiene and building requirements.
* Essential seating capacity and storage.
* Budget limits and opening deadlines.

### Keep selected elements flexible

These may evolve through sourcing:

* Decorative furniture and accent pieces.
* Artwork, mirrors and framed material.
* Small tables, shelving and display objects.
* Some lighting fixtures, where technically suitable.
* Menu covers, trays and tableware collections.

This approach gives discovery a meaningful role without allowing it to compromise the operation.

The goal is not to fit the business around every object you find. It is to use the right discoveries to make the business more distinctive.

## 08 — BUILD A COHERENT COLLECTION, NOT A STORAGE ROOM

The danger of flea-market design is accumulation. Every object may have a story, but not every story belongs in the same room.

A useful collection has rhythm and hierarchy. Some objects act as focal points; others provide texture or repetition. A distinctive cabinet might anchor a wall, while a group of similar glasses creates coherence across the tables.

Think about the collection in layers:

**The anchor pieces** establish the identity. These might be a counter, a large mirror, an exceptional table or a distinctive architectural element.

**The supporting pieces** reinforce the material and visual language. Chairs, lamps, shelving and smaller furniture belong here.

**The details** create depth at closer range: menus, trays, candleholders, framed prints, ceramics and small objects.

Not every surface needs to carry a story. Empty space allows important objects to be noticed and helps prevent the room from feeling cluttered.

Repetition can also make a varied collection feel intentional. Similar tones, materials, shapes or proportions can connect objects from different periods and sources.

The designer's task is to edit as carefully as they collect.

## 09 — THE BUSINESS VALUE OF A DISTINCTIVE INTERIOR

A salvage-led interior can create more than visual interest.

A coherent collection may help a venue become recognisable, encourage guests to remember specific details and give the team a stronger sense of identity. Objects can also provide material for storytelling, photography and communication.

But authenticity should not be treated as a marketing trick. Guests are increasingly able to distinguish between a genuinely considered environment and a collection of decorative references assembled only for photographs.

The business value comes from alignment.

If the venue promises warmth, familiarity and discovery, the objects should support those qualities. If the concept is restrained and contemporary, a few carefully selected antiques may be more effective than a room full of vintage pieces.

A distinctive interior also needs to function economically. Furniture must support the intended seating capacity, tableware must withstand repeated service, and decorative elements must be maintainable within the available staffing model.

The most successful solution is not necessarily the cheapest one or the most visually dramatic. It is the one that expresses the concept while remaining practical to operate.

## THE TAKEAWAY

Joi Espresso Bar and Denis Bobkov's salvage-led environments demonstrate how a clear point of view can create character without requiring an entirely new, expensive fit-out.

The process begins with a feeling, develops through careful sourcing and becomes coherent through editing. Flea markets, second-hand suppliers, auctions, salvage yards and repair specialists become part of the designer's creative toolkit.

The essential principles are:

* **Define the story before sourcing.** Let the concept guide every purchase.
* **Look beyond conventional suppliers.** Existing objects can offer character, material quality and opportunities for adaptation.
* **Preserve what is valuable.** Repair and repurpose when the result is safe, durable and economically sensible.
* **Calculate the real cost.** Include transport, labour, restoration and installation.
* **Edit with discipline.** A distinctive world is created through selection, not accumulation.

Money can accelerate procurement and improve finish. It cannot replace the clarity required to make different objects belong together.

## COURSE CONNECTION

This lesson translates the visual identity of your final hospitality concept into a tangible collection of objects and materials.

As you develop the venue mockup, consider which physical elements communicate its character most strongly: the counter, seating, lighting, tableware, menu artefact, doors, mirrors, artwork or smaller details guests encounter at close range.

Not every element needs to be newly manufactured. A combination of second-hand finds, restored pieces, custom-built components and carefully selected new equipment may express the concept more convincingly than a uniform catalogue-based interior.

The important distinction is between objects that simply look interesting and objects that reinforce the venue's underlying idea. Their sourcing, condition, scale and practical function should all support the same direction.

The final concept should also demonstrate that the aesthetic is achievable: the objects can be sourced or made, the budget accounts for their real costs, and the space remains safe and workable for guests and staff.

**A convincing hospitality world is not defined by how much was purchased. It is defined by how thoughtfully every element was chosen.**


# LESSON 11

# THEATRICAL TECHNIQUES & THE LIVE MOCKUP FROM FOUND OBJECTS

**Editorial lesson · 19 minutes**

## THE IDEA

### Look beyond the surface.

A hospitality concept should not be tested only on a flat blueprint, a digital rendering, or a model made from pristine white paper. It should be experienced as a physical world.

Theatre offers a powerful lesson for hospitality design: **atmosphere is constructed through carefully orchestrated details, but the audience should never see the machinery behind the illusion.** The texture of an old wooden table, the glow of a candle, the weight of a vintage glass, a fold of velvet, and a narrow beam of warm light can communicate more about a venue's identity than an elaborate architectural rendering.

Designers such as Denis Bobkov, whose work includes Abbey Players and Black Swan, and Egor Tarasenko, associated with Joi Espresso Bar, demonstrate the creative potential of working with characterful objects, layered materials, and an expressive point of view.

In this lesson, we move from the abstract concept to a tangible scene. Instead of representing your future venue with paper, assemble a live mockup from found objects, improvised materials, and a physical prototype of your menu. Place these elements together under real lighting, then observe what happens.

The objective is not to reproduce an entire restaurant or bar at miniature scale. It is to create one convincing fragment of its world: a table, a counter, a service ritual, or a corner that makes the concept feel real.

If someone sees the resulting photograph and immediately understands the atmosphere you are trying to create, the mockup is doing its job.

## 01 — THINK LIKE A THEATRE DESIGNER

Theatre does not begin with decoration. It begins with a story, a mood, and an understanding of what the audience should feel.

Hospitality design can follow the same principle. Before choosing a prop or material, define the emotional experience the space should communicate. Is it intimate and conspiratorial? Romantic and nostalgic? Raw, mysterious, playful, or quietly luxurious?

Once the intended atmosphere is clear, every object becomes part of the scenography.

* **Patina** introduces age, memory, and a sense of accumulated history.
* **Distressing** gives surfaces the appearance of use, wear, and time.
* **Glazing** changes the depth, reflection, and character of a finish.
* **Drapery** creates softness, enclosure, movement, and theatrical framing.
* **Trompe-l'œil** uses visual illusion to make a surface or object appear different from what it is.
* **Candlelight** introduces movement, intimacy, and changing shadows.
* **Focused lighting** directs attention toward a single object, ritual, or point of service.

These techniques are not a checklist of decorative effects. They are tools for communicating a specific idea.

A distressed wooden counter might suggest a place that has existed for generations. A translucent curtain might make a compact room feel private. A single illuminated coupe glass might turn a simple cocktail presentation into a memorable ritual.

The important distinction is between atmosphere and theatrical excess. A successful illusion supports the guest's experience without drawing attention to the trick itself.

## 02 — BUILD WITH FOUND OBJECTS, NOT PAPER

A conventional architectural mockup is useful for studying proportions and spatial relationships. But it rarely communicates the tactile and emotional qualities that define hospitality.

A live mockup answers different questions: How does the tableware feel together? What happens when candlelight meets the glass? Does the menu belong beside the plate? Does the combination of textures support the intended story?

Begin with objects that can be sourced, borrowed, repurposed, or improvised:

* Antique plates, bowls, and coupe glasses.
* Vintage glassware, bottles, and serving vessels.
* Candles and candleholders with distinct silhouettes.
* Scraps of velvet, linen, leather, or other textiles.
* Aged wood, stone, metal, or textured surfaces.
* Small decorative objects with a clear relationship to the concept.
* Herbs, spices, or botanical ingredients that connect the scene to its food and drink.
* A physical prototype of the venue's menu.

The goal is not to accumulate as many objects as possible. It is to identify a small group that works together.

An antique plate may establish the visual language of the table. A candleholder may introduce height and rhythm. A particular glass may connect the table setting to the signature drink. A botanical element may communicate the venue's ingredient philosophy.

Each object should contribute something identifiable to the overall composition.

**Found objects are not simply a budget alternative to new products. They are a design resource.** Their irregularities, wear, and previous lives can give a new venue a sense of depth that would be difficult to achieve through uniform, newly purchased objects alone.

## 03 — MAKE THE MENU PART OF THE SCENOGRAPHY

The menu should not be added to the scene at the last moment. It is one of the principal objects through which a guest encounters the concept.

Earlier lessons explored how menus can operate as manifestos, archives, collections, and physical artefacts. Here, that thinking becomes tangible.

Consider the editorial approaches of Rémy Savage, Bar Leone, El Copitas, and Tuju. Their differing approaches to identity and menu presentation offer a useful reminder that a menu can communicate far more than a list of products: it can express a venue's cultural references, personality, and relationship with its guests.

When building your mockup, place the menu directly into the composition. Consider its scale beside the tableware, its material against the fabric, the way its pages catch the light, and how a guest would naturally pick it up.

Ask three questions:

1. **Does the menu belong to this world?** Its typography, material, format, and language should feel consistent with the venue's identity.
2. **Does it invite interaction?** A guest should understand how to hold it, open it, read it, or engage with it.
3. **Does it complete the scene?** The menu should strengthen the relationship between the objects, the service ritual, and the overall atmosphere.

A beautifully staged table with an unrelated menu is not a coherent concept. It is a collection of attractive elements that have not yet become a system.

## 04 — LIGHT IS A MATERIAL

Lighting is not merely the final technical layer of a mockup. It changes the perceived character of every other material in the scene.

Candlelight can make glass feel warmer, deepen the shadows around an object, reveal the irregularities of an old surface, and create a sense of intimacy. A focused beam can isolate a menu or glass and make it feel almost ceremonial. Soft reflected light can make a rough material feel more inviting than direct illumination would.

The same collection of objects can communicate entirely different ideas under different lighting conditions.

For a live mockup, begin with the conditions your guest is most likely to experience. If your concept is an intimate evening bar, test it in warm, low light rather than under bright office lighting. If the venue is intended to feel open and botanical, consider how natural or diffuse light reveals the materials and ingredients.

Pay attention to the relationship between light and shadow. Darkness can create intimacy, but it can also hide the very details that make a design meaningful. A menu must remain readable. A drink must look appealing. A guest must be able to navigate the table comfortably.

Theatrical atmosphere should enhance hospitality, not compromise it.

For practical testing, use safe, stable candleholders and keep open flames away from fabric, paper, and other combustible materials. Where the intended venue would require a safer alternative, test suitable LED candles or other controlled lighting sources as well.

## 05 — COMPOSE THE SCENE FROM THE GUEST'S PERSPECTIVE

A mockup is only useful if it helps you understand what the guest will actually experience.

Once the objects are assembled, view the composition from seated eye height. This perspective is fundamentally different from looking down at a tabletop or photographing everything from above.

Notice what enters the frame first. Is the menu visible? Does the candle obstruct the view? Does the glass dominate the composition? Are the materials legible, or do they disappear into darkness? Does the arrangement feel naturally usable, or does it look like a still life designed exclusively for a photograph?

Think about the sequence of attention.

A guest may first notice the atmosphere of the room, then the table, then the menu, and finally the glass or object associated with the first drink. That sequence can be designed.

Photograph the mockup from the angles that best represent the experience:

* **The seated view:** What does a guest see upon approaching or sitting at the table?
* **The intimate detail:** How do the menu, glassware, materials, and light interact at close range?
* **The wider context:** How does the vignette relate to the surrounding space and communicate the venue's identity?

These views are not simply documentation. They become a visual argument for the concept, helping collaborators understand what the finished venue should feel like.

A compelling image should communicate the atmosphere without requiring a lengthy explanation.

## 06 — FROM AN ATTRACTIVE IMAGE TO A BUILDABLE IDEA

The live mockup is a creative tool, not a substitute for technical design.

Found objects can reveal relationships that are difficult to predict on screen, but the final venue must still work operationally. A beautiful candleholder may occupy too much table space. A delicate vintage glass may be unsuitable for a high-volume service. A textile may be visually effective but difficult to clean. An unstable object may create a safety risk.

These limitations are useful discoveries, not reasons to abandon the concept.

Separate the elements into three categories:

* **Keep:** objects and material relationships that express the concept and work practically.
* **Adapt:** elements that are visually successful but require changes in size, construction, finish, or maintenance.
* **Replace:** elements whose appearance is compelling but whose safety, durability, hygiene, or operational requirements make them unsuitable.

This process helps translate a theatrical prototype into a real hospitality environment.

It also creates a more precise brief for future sourcing and fabrication. Instead of asking a supplier for something that simply looks “vintage” or “romantic,” you can identify the qualities that matter: a particular silhouette, a warm reflective surface, a tactile finish, or a specific relationship between height and scale.

The mockup should preserve the emotional truth of the concept while allowing the final objects to be adapted to the realities of service.

## THE TAKEAWAY

A hospitality venue is experienced through physical details, sensory relationships, and carefully staged moments. A live mockup allows you to test these qualities before committing to construction or procurement.

Found objects bring material character and individuality. Theatrical techniques help control atmosphere. Lighting determines how the scene is perceived. A physical menu connects the venue's identity to the guest's first interaction with its offer. Photography reveals whether these elements work together from the perspective that matters most.

The objective is not to create a perfect miniature of the future venue. It is to construct one convincing piece of its world.

**When the objects, light, menu, and atmosphere tell the same story, the concept becomes tangible.**

## COURSE CONNECTION

This lesson brings the course's earlier ideas into a physical form. The concept manifesto establishes the story; the menu expresses its point of view; the furniture, merchandise, and service objects give that identity a material language; operational planning determines whether the experience can function in practice.

The live mockup connects these dimensions before they become a finished venue.

As the course moves toward the final hospitality concept and mockup, this approach provides a way to test the proposed world visually and materially. It helps establish which objects, textures, lighting conditions, and menu details should define the venue, while identifying practical constraints that must be resolved in the final design.

The resulting photographs and observations can become part of the project's visual presentation, communicating not only what the venue looks like, but also the atmosphere it promises, the details that create it, and the experience a guest is meant to remember.


# LESSON 13

# DESIGN THE HOSPITALITY CONCEPT OF TOMORROW

## OPEN & OPERATE

**Editorial lesson · 20 minutes**

## THE IDEA

### Turn the vision into a complete hospitality proposition.

A compelling hospitality concept is more than a beautiful room, an inventive menu, or a memorable service ritual. It is a connected system in which every decision — from the guest you want to attract to the way your team works behind the bar — contributes to one coherent experience.

This final lesson brings together the central ideas of the elective. You have explored sensory design, conceptual menus, designed objects, automation, artificial intelligence, ingredient research, business strategy, salvage-led interiors, and theatrical scenography. Now these ideas must work together as a single, credible proposition.

Imagine that you are preparing to open a hospitality venue. You must convince someone not only that it deserves to exist, but that you understand who it serves, why guests will choose it, how it will operate, what makes it distinctive, and how the experience can be realised within a defined budget.

This is the meaning of **Open & Operate**: the ability to imagine an extraordinary guest experience and translate it into a practical, financially grounded, and responsibly managed hospitality business.

Your final concept has three interconnected expressions:

* **The pitch deck:** the strategic and creative argument for the venue.
* **The physical Menu Concept:** an artefact that expresses the venue's identity, editorial voice, and approach to food and drink.
* **The live found-object mockup:** a tangible demonstration of the atmosphere, materials, table setting, lighting, and details the guest will encounter.

Each expression should reinforce the others. Together, they should make the venue understandable, desirable, and believable.

## 01 — DEFINE THE GUEST, THE OCCASION, AND THE USP

Every successful concept begins with a clear understanding of whom it serves and why that person would choose it.

Avoid defining your audience so broadly that the concept could appeal to anyone. Instead, describe a recognisable guest, their motivations, and the occasion that brings them through the door.

Are they meeting someone for a first date? Looking for a neighbourhood ritual after work? Exploring unfamiliar flavours? Celebrating a special occasion? Seeking an intimate place where the quality of the drink matters as much as the conversation?

The occasion helps determine the entire experience. It influences the price point, the duration of the visit, the menu structure, the atmosphere, the service rhythm, and even the furniture.

Once the audience and occasion are clear, articulate your unique selling proposition (USP) in one sentence.

A useful USP explains three things: what the venue offers, whom it serves, and what makes the experience meaningfully different.

For example:

*An intimate botanical cocktail bar where seasonal ingredients, Japanese-inspired precision, and theatrical tableside rituals transform an evening drink into a personal discovery.*

This is an illustrative direction, not a formula to copy. The final proposition should emerge from your own research and creative point of view.

The USP becomes the reference against which every subsequent decision can be tested. If an expensive design feature, a complicated preparation, or an elaborate service ritual does not strengthen the proposition, its place in the concept should be reconsidered.

**Clarity is not the enemy of creativity. It gives creativity a direction.**

## 02 — BUILD A COHERENT WORLD: SPACE, ATMOSPHERE, AND MEMORY

The venue should feel like one world rather than a collection of unrelated references.

Think about the guest's experience as a continuous narrative. What do they notice when approaching the entrance? What changes when they sit down? Which object attracts their attention? How does the first drink arrive? What do they remember when they leave?

The atmosphere should be recognisable across the architecture, materials, lighting, tableware, menu, sound, service, and language.

The theatrical approach explored through Denis Bobkov's work and the salvage-led philosophy associated with Joi Espresso Bar offer useful ways to develop this coherence. Found objects, patinated surfaces, vintage glassware, textiles, candlelight, and carefully composed details can create a sense of history and intimacy without requiring an extravagant construction budget.

Your live mockup should demonstrate this continuity. It should not simply show attractive objects; it should communicate the same emotional promise expressed in your pitch deck.

A useful distinction is between *visual consistency* and *experiential consistency*. Matching colours and materials may produce visual harmony, but the experience becomes convincing only when the service, menu, lighting, and objects also support the same idea.

Protect the atmosphere from details that break the illusion. An inappropriate menu format, harsh lighting, awkward table layout, visible clutter, or a service procedure that interrupts conversation can undermine the intended experience.

The strongest concepts anticipate these contradictions before opening.

## 03 — DESIGN THE MENU AS A CONCEPTUAL ARTEFACT

Your Menu Concept should be more than a list of drinks or dishes. It should make the venue's point of view tangible.

The approaches associated with Rémy Savage, Bar Leone, El Copitas, and Tuju offer different ways to consider how a menu can communicate identity, cultural context, research, and hospitality. The aim is not to imitate a particular venue, but to understand how an editorial idea becomes a guest-facing format.

Start with the central proposition of your menu. What does it explore? A landscape, a season, a cultural exchange, a collection of memories, a set of techniques, or a particular approach to flavour?

Then determine how the menu expresses that idea.

Consider:

* **Structure:** Is the menu organised by flavour, place, season, narrative, ingredient, or occasion?
* **Language:** Is the writing direct, poetic, playful, technical, or deliberately minimal?
* **Physical form:** What size, material, binding, or format suits the experience?
* **Guest navigation:** Can guests understand the offer and make confident choices?
* **Service integration:** How does the team introduce the menu, explain unfamiliar ingredients, and guide decisions?
* **Commercial logic:** Does the range support sensible preparation, ingredient reuse, pricing, and inventory control?

The menu should be distinctive without becoming inaccessible. Conceptual depth is valuable only when the guest can engage with it.

Its physical presentation should also connect to the live mockup. The menu belongs on the table, beside the glassware, under the intended lighting. It is part of the scene and part of the business.

## 04 — MAKE THE EXPERIENCE SENSORY AND MEMORABLE

A hospitality concept becomes memorable when its sensory details work together rather than compete for attention.

The work of Artem Talalay and Dave Arnold offers ways to think about flavour architecture, technical experimentation, and repeatable results. Hiroyasu Kayama's botanical approach at Bar Benfiddich highlights the expressive potential of ingredients, house-made preparations, and direct engagement with the natural world. Jiro Ono's shokunin philosophy offers another perspective: the pursuit of precision through disciplined attention to craft.

These references represent different approaches, but each points toward the same principle: every detail should have a reason to exist.

For each signature serve, consider the complete sensory experience:

* **Taste:** What is the central flavour idea, and how does the drink or dish achieve balance?
* **Aroma:** What does the guest smell before and during the first taste?
* **Texture:** How do temperature, carbonation, viscosity, glassware, or food texture affect perception?
* **Visual character:** What do colour, shape, garnish, and presentation communicate?
* **Touch and sound:** How do the vessel's weight, the surface beneath it, the sound of service, and the handling ritual contribute?

Neurogastronomy encourages designers to consider how perception is shaped by context, expectation, memory, and multiple senses. A particular vessel, scent, or presentation can influence how a guest interprets a flavour, although the effect will vary between people and situations.

Choose one or two sensory principles that genuinely strengthen your concept. Not every serve needs a complex theatrical ritual or advanced technical process. A precisely executed, apparently simple drink may express the venue more convincingly than a complicated creation without a clear purpose.

The final experience should feel intentional, repeatable, and appropriate to the occasion.

## 05 — DESIGN SERVICE AROUND PEOPLE, NOT JUST PERFORMANCE

Service is the point at which the concept becomes a relationship between the venue and the guest.

The approaches of Simone Caporale and Alex Kratena offer useful reference points for thinking about service choreography, bar formats, and the relationship between technical precision and human interaction.

Map the guest journey from arrival to departure. Consider how the guest is welcomed, seated, introduced to the menu, served, supported when making a decision, and invited to return.

Then examine the operational sequence behind each moment. What preparation must happen before service? Which tools are required? Where does the team move? Which tasks must occur simultaneously? Where can delays or unnecessary movement appear?

A beautiful ritual is only successful if it can be performed consistently under real service conditions.

Automation and technology should support this process rather than dominate it. Repetitive, predictable tasks may benefit from systems, equipment, or software. Human attention should remain available for hospitality that depends on judgement, empathy, communication, and adaptation.

Your concept should identify the appropriate balance between automation and human contact.

Also consider the working environment. Clear workflows, realistic staffing, safe equipment, adequate preparation time, fair scheduling, and appropriate training are not secondary operational concerns. They determine whether the intended experience can be sustained without exhausting the people who deliver it.

**The guest should experience effortless hospitality; the team should have a system that makes it possible.**

## 06 — CONNECT CREATIVITY TO A VIABLE BUSINESS MODEL

A concept can be creatively distinctive and still fail if its operating model does not support it.

The business model must explain how the venue earns revenue, manages costs, uses its space, and sustains its team.

The operating principles associated with Bek Narzi and Horeca Code, alongside the restaurant-group strategies of Boris Zarkov and White Rabbit Family, offer useful perspectives on the relationship between creative ambition and operational discipline.

Your financial logic should address the main drivers of the business.

**Revenue and capacity**

* How many guests can the venue accommodate comfortably?
* What is the expected average check?
* How many seat turns or transactions are realistic for the format?
* Which days and service periods are likely to drive demand?
* How does the concept encourage repeat visits without relying entirely on novelty?

**Costs and resources**

* What are the estimated ingredient and beverage costs?
* What labour and preparation time does the menu require?
* What are the rent, utilities, equipment, maintenance, and other fixed costs?
* Which elements require upfront investment?
* How much working capital is needed to support the opening period?

**Operating performance**

* What gross margin does the menu need to support?
* How will inventory, waste, purchasing, and portion consistency be controlled?
* Which indicators will show whether the venue is performing as expected?
* What happens if sales are lower or costs higher than forecast?

Do not confuse a financial estimate with a proven result. Make assumptions explicit and use credible evidence where available. A clear model with transparent assumptions is more useful than a precise-looking forecast built on guesswork.

The creative concept and the business model must inform one another. If a signature serve requires expensive ingredients, extensive preparation, and specialist labour, its price, volume, and operational role need to reflect those demands.

Commercial discipline should protect the creative idea by making it sustainable.

## 07 — CHOOSE YOUR INFLUENCES WITH INTENTION

The course has introduced a range of practitioners whose work can inform different parts of a hospitality concept. The final project does not need to incorporate every reference. In fact, trying to do so may weaken the venue's identity.

Choose one or two principles that are genuinely relevant to your proposition, then explain how they influence your decisions.

<ins>Hiroyasu Kayama — farm-to-glass thinking</ins>

Use botanical knowledge, seasonal ingredients, and house-made preparations to create a direct relationship between origin and flavour.

<ins>Denis Bobkov — salvage scenography</ins>

Use found objects, patina, repair, and theatrical composition to establish character and atmosphere through material choices.

# PRACTICAL ASSIGNMENTS

## From Creative Research to Open & Operate

Each assignment develops one part of the student's final hospitality concept. The work should accumulate throughout the course, allowing the final submission to bring together the concept, menu, guest experience, operating model, sourcing strategy, and physical mockup.

---

## LESSON 03 — THE SENSES AT THE TABLE, VESSEL WEIGHT & FLAVOUR ARCHITECTURE

**Assignment 03: The Sensory Serve**

**Objective**

Develop one signature drink or dish through sensory research, demonstrating how flavour, aroma, texture, colour, and presentation work together.

**Instructions**

1. Define the central idea of one signature serve and the experience it should create.
2. Map its sensory characteristics: taste, aroma, texture, colour, temperature, and mouthfeel.
3. Select two or three possible vessels or serving formats and explain how each changes the perceived experience.
4. Create a simple tasting and presentation prototype. Record observations about balance, aroma, visual impact, and usability.
5. Explain how the serve reflects the intended identity of your future venue.

**Submission**

* One-page sensory concept sheet.
* A sensory map of the signature serve.
* Reference images or photographs of the prototype.
* A short explanation of the vessel choice and its role in the experience.

**Deliverable:** One signature serve with a clear sensory rationale.

---

## LESSON 04 — CONCEPT MANIFESTOS & DECONSTRUCTING THE WORLD’S 50 BEST MENUS

**Assignment 04: The Menu Manifesto**

**Objective**

Create a conceptual foundation for a menu that expresses a distinctive hospitality identity rather than simply listing products.

**Instructions**

1. Write a short concept manifesto describing the venue's point of view, cultural references, intended audience, and emotional promise.
2. Research three relevant menus or hospitality concepts, including examples that demonstrate different editorial approaches.
3. Analyse their structure, language, visual identity, guest navigation, and relationship to the venue's story.
4. Identify the principles you want to adopt, adapt, or deliberately reject.
5. Develop a preliminary menu architecture: for example, by ingredient, flavour, season, place, narrative, or occasion.
6. Explain how the menu will make the venue recognisable without copying an existing business.

**Submission**

* Concept manifesto.
* Three short menu case-study analyses.
* A proposed menu structure.
* A visual reference board.

**Deliverable:** A clear editorial direction for the venue's Menu Concept.

---

## LESSON 05 — CONCEPT OBJECTS: THE MENU, THE MERCHANDISE & THE FURNITURE

**Assignment 05: The Object Family**

**Objective**

Translate the venue's concept into a coherent family of physical objects that guests encounter and use.

**Instructions**

1. Select three objects that are central to the guest experience: a menu, a service object, and a furniture or merchandise element.
2. Define the purpose of each object and the moment in the guest journey when it appears.
3. Choose appropriate materials, finishes, dimensions, and visual references.
4. Consider touch, durability, cleaning, storage, repair, and replacement.
5. Estimate a realistic unit cost or sourcing range for each object.
6. Explain how the objects relate visually and conceptually to one another.

**Submission**

* Three object concept sheets.
* Material and reference board.
* Preliminary dimensions and material specifications.
* Estimated sourcing or production costs.

**Deliverable:** A connected object family that expresses the venue's identity and can be developed for real use.

---

## LESSON 06 — AUTOMATION, ERGONOMICS & THE HORECA CODE

**Assignment 06: The Invisible Operating System**

**Objective**

Design a basic operational workflow that supports excellent hospitality without creating unnecessary complexity for the team.

**Instructions**

1. Map the preparation and service sequence for one signature menu item.
2. Identify repetitive tasks, precision-critical tasks, and moments that require human judgement.
3. Mark where tools, equipment, software, or automation could improve consistency or reduce unnecessary work.
4. Sketch the relevant workstation and indicate the movement of people, ingredients, tools, and finished orders.
5. Identify likely bottlenecks, safety concerns, and ergonomic problems.
6. Explain how the proposed workflow protects time for guest interaction.

**Submission**

* A workflow diagram.
* A simple workstation layout.
* An automation-versus-human-attention matrix.
* A short list of operational risks and proposed improvements.

**Deliverable:** A practical service workflow that balances efficiency, craft, ergonomics, and human contact.

---

## LESSON 07 — AI AS A CREATIVE AND OPERATIONAL TOOL

**Assignment 07: The Human–AI Development Brief**

**Objective**

Use AI to support concept development while retaining human judgement, authorship, and responsibility.

**Instructions**

1. Select one real task within your hospitality concept, such as menu research, naming, flavour exploration, demand forecasting, or preparation planning.
2. Write a clear brief describing the task, the context, the constraints, and the desired result.
3. Use an AI tool to generate or analyse possible approaches.
4. Document the most useful results, the inaccuracies or weaknesses you identified, and the changes you made.
5. Distinguish between AI-generated suggestions, independently verified information, and your own creative decisions.
6. Write a short AI-use policy covering privacy, factual verification, creative ownership, and appropriate human oversight.

**Submission**

* Original brief and selected AI outputs.
* A short decision log showing what was retained, revised, or rejected.
* Final human-edited result.
* A concise AI ethics charter.

**Deliverable:** A documented example of responsible AI-assisted work relevant to the venue.

## LESSON 08 — THE NEXT TABLE & HIROYASU KAYAMA’S FARM-TO-GLASS APOTHECARY

**Assignment 08: From Origin to Memory**

**Objective**

Develop a signature serve or menu element around an ingredient's origin, seasonality, sensory character, and potential to create a memorable guest experience.

**Instructions**

1. Choose one principal ingredient with a clear connection to a place, season, producer, or personal story.
2. Research its origin, availability, flavour profile, and relevant sourcing considerations.
3. Explore at least two preparation methods, such as infusion, fermentation, clarification, dehydration, or another suitable technique.
4. Compare the results in terms of flavour, texture, aroma, consistency, resource use, and practical feasibility.
5. Develop a preliminary signature serve or menu element around the most promising approach.
6. Explain how the ingredient's story will be communicated honestly to guests.

Use appropriate food-safety practices and do not attempt specialist techniques without suitable equipment, training, and controls.

**Submission**

* Ingredient origin and research sheet.
* Preparation and experimentation notes.
* Sensory comparison.
* Signature serve or menu-element concept.
* Short sourcing and seasonality rationale.

**Deliverable:** One ingredient-led concept connecting origin, technique, flavour, and guest memory.

## LESSON 09 — FROM IDEA TO OPERATING MODEL & TALENT ECOSYSTEM

**Assignment 09: The First Operating Model**

**Objective**

Test whether the proposed venue has a credible commercial structure and a realistic plan for developing its team.

**Instructions**

1. Define the venue format, capacity, opening schedule, target audience, and service periods.
2. Estimate the average check, expected guest volume, and principal revenue streams.
3. Identify the major cost categories, including ingredients, labour, rent, utilities, equipment, and other operating expenses.
4. State your assumptions clearly and estimate the sales level required to cover the principal costs.
5. Identify three to five operating indicators that would help you assess performance.
6. Create a preliminary team structure, identifying key responsibilities and essential training needs.
7. Outline the priorities for the first 90 days, from preparation and staff training to opening and review.

**Submission**

* One-page operating model.
* Preliminary revenue and cost assumptions.
* Basic break-even estimate.
* Team and responsibility chart.
* 90-day launch outline.

**Deliverable:** A first-pass business and talent plan that connects creative ambition to operating reality.

---

## LESSON 10 — SOUL BEFORE BUDGET: JOI ESPRESSO BAR & DENIS BOBKOV’S SALVAGE WORLDS

**Assignment 10: The Salvage & Sourcing Plan**

**Objective**

Develop a material and object-sourcing strategy that gives the venue character while remaining financially and operationally credible.

**Instructions**

1. Define the atmosphere and material language of the venue using a concise visual reference board.
2. Identify three anchor objects that could establish the character of the space.
3. Add supporting objects and smaller details that reinforce the same visual world.
4. Research potential sourcing routes, including second-hand markets, salvage suppliers, existing stock, repair, reuse, and new production where appropriate.
5. Estimate acquisition, transport, restoration, cleaning, and maintenance costs.
6. Identify which objects are suitable for direct reuse and which may need adaptation or replacement for hygiene, durability, accessibility, or safety.
7. Compare the estimated salvage-led approach with a reasonable alternative sourcing scenario.

**Submission**

* Salvage and material moodboard.
* Annotated list of proposed objects.
* Preliminary sourcing and budget table.
* Notes on restoration, maintenance, safety, and operational suitability.

**Deliverable:** A credible sourcing plan showing how the venue's character can be realised within a defined budget.

---

## LESSON 11 — THEATRICAL TECHNIQUES & THE LIVE MOCKUP FROM FOUND OBJECTS

**Assignment 11: The Live Found-Object Mockup**

**Objective**

Translate the venue's visual identity into a physical scene using found objects, a menu prototype, and considered lighting.

**Instructions**

1. Select a small area of the intended venue to represent, such as a table, counter, service station, or intimate corner.
2. Assemble real objects that support the concept: tableware, glassware, candles or suitable lighting alternatives, textiles, wood, and other materials.
3. Include a physical prototype of the Menu Concept.
4. Compose the objects so that their materials, shapes, and relationships communicate one clear story.
5. Test the scene under lighting appropriate to the intended guest experience.
6. Photograph the mockup from the guest's seated perspective, including a wider view and close-up details.
7. Review the result for visual coherence and practical concerns, such as readability, stability, cleaning, and safe use.

Use safe candle practices and keep flames away from combustible materials.

**Submission**

* Photographs of the live mockup from at least three viewpoints.
* A short explanation of the concept and object choices.
* A list of the principal materials and proposed sourcing routes.
* A brief reflection on what the mockup revealed and what would need to change before implementation.

**Deliverable:** A physical, photographed demonstration of the venue's atmosphere and material identity.

## LESSON 13 — DESIGN THE HOSPITALITY CONCEPT OF TOMORROW: OPEN & OPERATE

**Assignment 13: The Final Open & Operate Pitch**

**Objective**

Bring the course's creative, spatial, sensory, operational, and commercial work together into one coherent hospitality concept.

**Instructions**

Prepare a final presentation that explains the venue as both a guest experience and an operating business.

Include:

1. **Concept and positioning:** venue name, target guest, occasion, and one-sentence USP.
2. **Experience narrative:** the intended atmosphere, guest journey, and defining service moments.
3. **Menu Concept:** editorial direction, physical format, and representative signature offers.
4. **Sensory design:** the role of flavour, aroma, texture, colour, vessel choice, and presentation.
5. **Space and objects:** material palette, furniture, lighting, and key designed objects.
6. **Live mockup:** photographs of the found-object scene and its relationship to the concept.
7. **Operating model:** capacity, average check assumptions, revenue streams, major costs, and performance indicators.
8. **Service and technology:** workflow, ergonomics, automation, and the role of human attention.
9. **Sourcing and budget:** salvage strategy, procurement assumptions, and estimated realisation costs.
10. **People and responsibility:** staffing principles, training, safe working conditions, sourcing responsibility, and relevant operational risks.
11. **Reference principles:** identify one or two practitioners whose ideas influenced your decisions and explain how you adapted those principles.
12. **Implementation priorities:** identify the major steps required to move from concept to opening.

Make the relationships between the sections explicit. The menu should support the USP; the service model should support the menu; the budget should support the design; and the mockup should demonstrate the atmosphere promised by the presentation.

Clearly label estimates and assumptions. Do not present unverified projections as established results.

**Submission**

* Complete Open & Operate pitch deck.
* Physical or photographed Menu Concept.
* Sourcing and budget plan.
* Photographs of the live found-object mockup.
* Supporting operating-model and service-workflow materials.

**Deliverable:** A complete hospitality concept and mockup that communicates a distinctive guest experience, a coherent design language, and a credible plan for responsible operation.

## HOW THE ASSIGNMENTS CONNECT

The assignments are designed to build on one another:

* **Lessons 03–04:** establish the sensory signature and conceptual voice.
* **Lessons 05–06:** translate the identity into objects and workable systems.
* **Lesson 07:** integrate AI thoughtfully into research and development.
* **Lesson 08:** develop ingredient-led creativity and a credible origin story.
* **Lesson 09:** test the operating model and team structure.
* **Lesson 10:** establish the sourcing strategy and budget.
* **Lesson 11:** make the concept tangible through a live mockup.
* **Lesson 13:** bring the accumulated work together in the final Open & Operate proposition.

The final project should not feel like a collection of separate exercises. It should demonstrate how each decision contributes to the same hospitality experience — from the first impression to the final operating detail.
